Late November is a good time to plan a balcony garden, mostly because nothing is growing and you can see the space plainly. The pots are empty or stacked in a corner, the light is low, and you have all winter to work out what will fit.

Most balcony failures come from the space rather than the gardener. Too little sun for tomatoes, pots that dry out twice a day in July, a gust that takes the beans over the edge. All of it can be sorted on paper first.

## Start with the sun you really get

This is the one that decides everything else. Tomatoes, peppers and beans want six or more hours of direct sun a day to fruit well. Lettuce, spinach, chard and most herbs manage on less, and some of them prefer a break from the afternoon heat.

Winter sun is lower and shorter, so a count taken now undersells what you'll get in June. Note which direction the balcony faces and what blocks it: the floor above, a neighboring building, the solid wall of the balcony itself, which can shade a pot on the floor even when the railing is in full sun. A south- or west-facing balcony with an open view is tomato country. A north-facing one is salad and herb country, and that is a perfectly good garden.

I spent one summer coaxing a tomato in a corner that only got morning sun. It flowered, sulked, and gave me about nine tomatoes. The lettuce in the same spot would have been happier.

## Weight, rules and the neighbor downstairs

Potting mix is heavy, and wet potting mix is a lot heavier. A row of big pots after a downpour weighs more than most people expect, so if you rent or live in a block, ask the building manager or read your lease before buying large containers. Some buildings limit what can go on balconies at all, and some ban anything hung over the railing.

Place the heaviest pots against the building wall rather than out at the edge, use plastic, fiberglass or fabric pots instead of terracotta where weight matters, and fill only as deep as the crop needs.

Then think about the person below. Water drains out of every pot, and it carries soil with it. Saucers under everything, and water slowly enough that nothing runs over the edge.

## Pot sizes, crop by crop

Small pots are the most common reason balcony vegetables struggle, because they dry out within hours on a hot day. Go bigger than feels necessary.

A cherry tomato wants at least five gallons, about 20 liters, and more is better. A pepper is happy in three to five gallons. Lettuce, spinach and other salad greens do fine in a trough or window box around six inches deep, and most herbs grow well in a pot of a similar depth, with mint kept in its own pot so it can't take over. Climbing beans want a deep box at the foot of something to climb.

Wind changes the choice too. Higher floors get more of it, and it dries pots fast and snaps tall plants. Wide, heavy-bottomed containers stay put, and a tomato is better staked low and sturdy than trained up tall where the gusts catch it.

## Use the railing, and plan the watering now

Floor space runs out first, so use the height. Climbing beans will go up a railing or a string trellis tied to it, and they crop for weeks from a single deep box. Railing planters hold herbs and salad without taking any floor at all, as long as your building allows them and they're fixed securely on the inside.

Watering is the part people underestimate. A balcony in July can need watering every day, and if the only tap is in the kitchen, that's a lot of trips with a can. Self-watering containers, the kind with a reservoir in the base, are worth the money here because they stretch the time between waterings. Work out now where the water will come from and how you'll carry it, before there are twenty pots relying on you.

**Pro tip.** Count the sun before you buy anything. Note when direct sun hits the balcony floor and when it leaves, then pick crops to fit those hours.
