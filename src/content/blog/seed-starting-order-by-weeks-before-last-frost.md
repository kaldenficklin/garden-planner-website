The seed catalogues will start landing in a month or so, and sometime in
January you'll sit down with a cup of tea and a pile of packets and try to
work out what goes in first. Every packet gives you a number of weeks. None of
them tell you how that number fits with the eight other packets on the table.

So here they are in one place, in the order they go in. Every number is weeks
before your average last spring frost, which is the date everything else
counts back from. If you don't know yours yet,
[find it](/blog/how-to-find-your-frost-dates/) before you do anything else on
this list, because a two-week error in that date shifts every row.

| Rank | Crop | Start indoors, weeks before last frost | Goes outside |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Onions and leeks from seed | 10 to 12 | A few weeks before last frost |
| 2 | Celery and celeriac | 10 to 12 | Around last frost |
| 3 | Peppers | 8 to 10 | Once nights stay warm, after last frost |
| 4 | Eggplant | 8 to 10 | Once nights stay warm, after last frost |
| 5 | Broccoli and cabbage | 6 to 8 | Two to four weeks before last frost |
| 6 | Tomatoes | 6 to 8 | After last frost |
| 7 | Lettuce | 4 to 6 | A few weeks before last frost |
| 8 | Basil | 4 to 6 | Well after last frost |
| 9 | Squash, cucumbers and melons | 3 to 4 | After last frost, into warm soil |

These are the ranges that show up again and again on seed packets and in
seed-starting charts, and they work for most gardens. Your house matters too.
A cool spare room under lights grows things more slowly than a warm kitchen,
so if your seedlings always seem to be ready late, shift toward the earlier
end of each range.

## The early ones: onions, leeks and celery

These go in first because they grow slowly for a long time. An onion seedling
is a single blade of grass for weeks. It's not in any hurry, and it doesn't
mind being cut back with scissors to a few inches tall when it gets floppy,
which keeps it sturdy.

Celery is the fussier of the two groups. The seed is tiny, it needs light to
germinate so it barely gets covered, and it can take a couple of weeks to show
itself. Start it on the early side and be patient.

## The ones people start too late: peppers and eggplant

Peppers are where most first-time seed starters go wrong in the other
direction from everything else. They start them at the same time as the
tomatoes, and the tomatoes race ahead while the peppers sit there.

Pepper seed germinates slowly in a cool room. It likes the mix warm, around
80°F, and at ordinary room temperature it can take two or three weeks to come
up. Then the seedlings grow slowly for their first month. So they need their
extra fortnight, and a heat mat under the tray helps more for peppers than for
anything else on this list. Eggplant behaves much the same and goes in at the
same time.

## The ones that go out before the last frost

Broccoli, cabbage, lettuce and onions are planted out while there's still a
risk of frost, because they can take a light one once they've been hardened
off. That's why broccoli and cabbage sit higher on the list than tomatoes even
though they grow faster. They need to be ready weeks earlier.

Lettuce is the one I'd say is optional to start indoors at all. It can be
sown straight into the bed in early spring and does well. I start a few under
lights anyway, just to have something green to plant out on the first mild
weekend, which is more for my morale than the lettuce.

## Tomatoes, in the middle where everyone puts them

Six to eight weeks, and if anything, resist the urge to go earlier. A tomato
seedling started ten or twelve weeks ahead will be tall, root-bound and
flowering in its pot by the time it's safe to plant out, and it often sulks
for weeks after transplanting while a younger one overtakes it. Every year I
think I'll get ahead by starting them early, and every year the later sowing
does as well or better.

## The late ones: basil, squash and cucumbers

Basil hates cold more than tomatoes do and will turn black at temperatures a
tomato shrugs off, so it goes out last and doesn't need to be started early.

Squash, cucumbers and melons grow so fast, and dislike having their roots
disturbed so much, that three or four weeks indoors is plenty. Start them in
individual pots big enough that you won't need to pot them up, and plant the
whole thing out without breaking up the root ball. A lot of people skip
starting these indoors altogether and sow them straight into warm soil, which
works well once the weather has settled.

## Doing the counting

The maths isn't hard, just tedious. Take your last frost date, count back ten
weeks for the onions and peppers, eight for the tomatoes, and so on, and write
the dates on the calendar.
