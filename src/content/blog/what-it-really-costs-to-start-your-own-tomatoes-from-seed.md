The seed catalogues are starting to land, and with them comes the question
every grocery-minded gardener eventually asks at the kitchen table: is
starting your own tomatoes from seed cheaper, or is it a hobby that tells
itself it's thrift?

It can be cheaper. Most years it is, for me. But the saving doesn't come from
where people expect it to, and in the first year it often isn't there at all.
The seed costs next to nothing. The light, the trays and six weeks of your
attention are the real price, and whether they pay depends on how many plants
you want and how many springs you use the same kit for.

## The seed is the cheap part

A packet of tomato seed usually costs about the same as one nursery
transplant, sometimes less. Inside you'll get anywhere from a dozen seeds to a
few dozen, depending on the variety and the seller (hybrids tend to come in
smaller counts). Kept cool and dry in a jar, tomato seed stays good for
several years, so one packet can cover more than one spring.

Do that sum once and it's hard to unsee. One packet is roughly one nursery
plant, and one packet is also enough for every tomato you'll grow for a couple
of seasons. Per plant, the seed is a rounding error.

And if you grow an open-pollinated variety, you can
[save seed from your best fruit](/blog/the-one-tomato-trick-that-means-you-never-buy-that-seed-packet-again/)
and stop buying it altogether.

## The kit is where the money goes

What costs money is everything a tomato seedling needs to grow indoors for the
six to eight weeks before your last frost. That means light, something to grow
in, seed-starting mix and, for some people, a heat mat to get the seed going.

Light is the big one. A windowsill on its own rarely gives tomato seedlings
enough, and what you get is a tray of tall, pale stems leaning at the glass. A
plain shop light hung a few inches above the tray does the job, and it doesn't
need to be anything fancy (the
[grow light post](/blog/your-seedlings-dont-need-a-200-dollar-grow-light-they-need-it-six-inches-closer/)
goes into why distance matters more than the spec). Trays and cell packs can
be washed and reused for years. The mix is the one thing you buy fresh every
spring.

This is where people get burned. If you buy a light and trays this winter and
grow four tomato plants, those four plants have cost you more than four
nursery plants would have. Usually quite a lot more. Plenty of people try it
once, do that sum in May, feel a bit foolish and never try again.

## Spread the light over the years you'll use it

The light is a one-off cost that pretends to be this year's cost. Next spring
it's already paid for. The spring after that, the same.

It also doesn't care what's under it. The light that grows your tomatoes
grows the peppers, the onions, the basil and a flat of lettuce for the first
bed. So the fairer way to cost a tomato seedling is to take what the light and
trays cost you, divide it by the number of springs you can reasonably expect
to use them and by everything you'll start under them, then add the mix and
the seed. My shop light is older than my car, and its cost per seedling by
now is too small to bother measuring.

Then put that next to what your own garden centre charges for the same number
of plants. Prices vary far too much from one place to the next for me to give
you a figure, which is why it's worth ten minutes with your own receipts.

There's electricity too. A shop light running most of the day for a couple of
months is a modest bill, but it isn't zero. The fixture lists its wattage, and
your bill lists your rate.

## What you pay that isn't money

Starting seed takes daily attention for six to eight weeks: checking the
water, raising the light, potting up once, then a week of
[hardening off](/blog/how-to-harden-off-seedlings/) before anything goes in
the ground. Some years you lose a tray to damping off, or to a cat, or to a
warm afternoon on the porch that turned into a cold night.

A nursery plant has had all of that done for it by someone whose job it is.
That counts for something, and it's fair to put a value on your own March.

## What seed gets you that the nursery can't

This is the part of the sum that doesn't show up on a receipt. A garden
centre stocks a handful of tomato varieties, chosen because they sell. A seed
catalogue lists hundreds.

If you garden to cut the grocery bill, that choice is worth a lot. A paste
tomato for sauce, a cherry that ripens early in a short season, a variety that
copes with the disease that wiped you out last August: those decide how much
food you get, and most of them never reach a nursery bench. The cheapest
tomato plant in the world is no bargain if it's the wrong tomato for how you
cook.

## When buying plants is the right call

If you want two or three tomato plants, buy them. The sums will never favour a
light for three plants unless you're starting other things under it. The same
goes if you have nowhere to hang a light, or your spring is already more than
you can manage, or this is your first garden and you'd rather learn to grow
the plant before you learn to raise it.

There's a middle path, too. A packet has far more seed than one household
needs, so split it with a neighbour, and the light as well if they're up for
it. Or buy your transplants this spring and start only tomatoes from seed the
year after, once you know you like the work.

Whichever way you go, write down what the plants gave you. If you log your
harvests in [Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278), it
keeps a running seasonal-savings total, which makes next winter's decision a
lot less of a guess.

And if you're still on the fence, buy one packet this winter and, when your
sowing date comes round, sow six seeds on your brightest windowsill, alongside the nursery plants you'd have bought
anyway. Six stretched seedlings will tell you more about whether a light is
worth it than any sum on the back of an envelope.
