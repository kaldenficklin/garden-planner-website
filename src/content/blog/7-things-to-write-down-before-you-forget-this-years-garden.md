Right now, at the end of November, you could probably walk me round your
garden and tell me what grew in every bed. Where the squash sprawled. Which
tomato split after the August rain. The corner where nothing much happened.

By the time the seed catalogues are spread over the kitchen table in January,
most of that will be gone. I know because I have planned more than one spring
around a garden I half remembered, and I've planted brassicas back into the
same bed two years running without meaning to. So this is the list I try to
get on paper before the first real snow, while it's still easy. It's seven
things, and you can do most of it in one sitting with a cup of tea.

## 1. What grew where

Draw the beds, roughly, and write in what was in each one. It doesn't need to
be to scale or pretty. If a bed held two crops through the season, an early
one and a late one, put both.

This is the note you'll use most. Crop rotation depends on it entirely, and
it's the thing nearly everybody forgets first, which is the whole argument of
[why rotation fails for most people](/blog/crop-rotation-only-works-if-you-remember-last-year-and-almost-nobody-does/).
Your memory of where the tomatoes were will feel certain in January and be
wrong about one bed in three.

## 2. The varieties, by name

Not "the cherry tomato". The name on the packet, and where you got it. Then a
line on each: grew well, sulked, tasted of nothing, cropped for months,
bolted in a week.

Varieties are the easiest thing in the garden to change and the hardest to
remember. If you grew two lettuces side by side and one held in the heat and
one didn't, that's worth more than anything you'll read in a catalogue
description. Write down the losers too, so you don't order them again because
the photo looked nice.

## 3. The dates that happened

When you sowed, when things went out, when the first harvest came and when
the last one did. Also your real frosts: the last one in spring and the first
one this autumn, as they happened in your garden rather than as the chart said.

These are the dates you'll be guessing at next year otherwise. The gap
between when a crop went in and when it gave you something is the number
that lets you plan a succession or squeeze in a fall sowing. And a first frost
that came two weeks earlier than you expected is something you want to
remember next September, when the beans are still going.

## 4. Too much and too little

Which crops left you giving bags away, and which ones ran out while you still
wanted more. A rough sense is enough: "zucchini, far too much", "garlic, gone
by October".

This is how quantities get fixed. Most of us plant the same number of plants
each year out of habit, and the glut and the shortage come back with it. One
honest line per crop means next year's seed order is for the amounts your
household eats.

## 5. What attacked what, and when

The pests and diseases that showed up, which crop, and roughly when you first
noticed. Cabbage caterpillars in July. Blight on the tomatoes by late August.
Slugs wiping out the first sowing of beans.

Many garden problems come back at about the same point in the season, and if
you know when they arrived this year you can be ready a week or two early
next year with a net, a row cover or a fresh sowing. It's also how you notice
that a problem keeps turning up in the same bed.

## 6. The spots that surprised you

The patch that stayed wet after rain. The bed that lost its sun by mid
afternoon once the neighbor's tree leafed out. Where the frost settled first.
Where things grew better than they had any right to.

These are hard to see in winter, when the trees are bare and the sun is low
and everything is just mud. Write them down now and they'll stop you putting
the peppers back in the shady corner because it looked sunny in March.

## 7. The jobs you never got to

The fence that needs fixing, the bed that wants more compost, the trellis that
fell over in a storm, the tool with the broken handle. Everything you said
you'd sort out "after the season".

The winter is long and these jobs are easy when the garden is asleep. They're
impossible in May. Put the list somewhere you'll see it, and if anything on it
means buying something, you've also got the start of a shopping list for the
quiet months.

## Where to keep it

A notebook in a kitchen drawer works, as long as you can find it next year. I
use the garden journal in
[Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278), mostly because
it keeps each year's notes alongside the ones before, and after a couple of
seasons that history starts telling you things a single year can't.

However you do it, do it soon. Every week the season goes a bit further out
of focus, and the thing you'll need most in January is whatever detail seems
too obvious to bother writing down tonight.
