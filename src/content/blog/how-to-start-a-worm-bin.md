November is when the compost heap goes quiet. It still takes the peelings, but nothing much happens to them until spring, and the bucket by the back door fills faster than the heap can break anything down.

A worm bin keeps going. Tucked into a utility room, or a garage that doesn't freeze, a small box of worms will turn a household's vegetable scraps into castings all winter, and it takes up about as much floor as a laundry basket.

## Red wigglers, and why your garden worms won't do

The worms you turn up in the vegetable bed are burrowers. They live deep, pull leaves down into their tunnels, and in a shallow box full of food they sulk, try to escape, or die.

Composting worms are a different species with a different life. Red wigglers (Eisenia fetida) live in the top layer of rotting leaves and manure, they eat fast, and they breed happily in a crowded bin. Some bait shops sell them and plenty of suppliers post them. If you order by mail in winter, pick a week when nobody's going to leave the parcel on a frozen step.

My first bin started with worms from a friend's heap, a coffee can full of them and quite a lot of their dinner. That works too, if you know someone with a busy compost pile.

## The box and the bedding

Any opaque plastic tub with a lid will do, the shallow kind sold for storage under a bed. Worms want dark and they want air, so drill a row of small holes high up on the sides and a few in the bottom for drainage. Stand it on a couple of bricks over a tray to catch whatever drips out.

Bedding is the worms' house, and they eat it slowly as well. Shredded newspaper, torn brown cardboard, egg boxes, a few dead leaves. Soak it, squeeze it out, and fluff it into the bin loosely so there's air between the pieces. A handful of garden soil gives them some grit and gets the right microbes going.

The moisture test is the same one you'd use on compost. Pick up a handful and squeeze. A drop or two should come out, like a sponge you've wrung out. Wetter than that and the bin goes sour and smelly. Drier, and the worms suffer, since they breathe through their damp skin.

## Feed less than you think

Most worm bins that fail were overfed. A new colony can't eat much, and food that sits uneaten rots, smells, and brings in fruit flies.

Start with a small handful of chopped scraps buried under the bedding in one corner. Check it after a few days. When most of it has gone, feed again in a different spot. As the worms multiply over the months, you'll find they can handle more.

Vegetable peelings, fruit cores, coffee grounds, tea leaves and crushed eggshells all go in. Leave out meat, fish, dairy, and anything oily or salty, which rot badly and draw pests. Go easy on citrus peel and onion. A little is usually fine, but worms tend to leave a big pile of either alone until it goes off.

## Cold garages and winter

Worms slow right down as the bin cools and pick up again when it warms. A garage that stays above freezing is fine. A bin that freezes solid is the end of the colony, so if yours gets close, bring it indoors for the cold snap or wrap it in old blankets against the house wall. They eat less in the cold, so feed less, and resist the urge to check on them every day.

## Getting the castings out

After a few months the bedding turns into something dark and crumbly that smells like a forest floor. Those are the castings.

The least fiddly way to separate them is to stop feeding one half of the bin. Push everything to one side, put fresh damp bedding and food in the empty half, and wait a few weeks. Most of the worms move over to the food, and you can scoop out the finished side with only a few stragglers, which go back in.

Castings are rich. Use them as a top dressing on pots, or mix a little into potting compost in spring, rather than filling a container with them. I keep a bag of them in the shed for planting day, and a scoop in the bottom of each planting hole is the best use I've found for them.

**Pro tip.** Keep a layer of dry shredded paper over the top of the bedding. It soaks up extra moisture, hides the food, and keeps fruit flies from finding it.
