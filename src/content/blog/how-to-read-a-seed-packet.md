Seed packets mostly get read after the seed is bought, standing at the bed with a trowel. Read them in winter instead, while you're ordering, and they'll tell you whether a variety suits your garden before you've spent a penny on it.

The front is there to sell you something. The useful part is on the back, and it's only a handful of lines.

## Days to maturity

This is how long the crop takes to be ready to harvest, and the catch is where the counting starts. For crops usually sown straight into the ground, like beans or carrots, it normally runs from sowing. For crops usually started indoors and transplanted, like tomatoes and peppers, it normally runs from the day the plant goes into the garden, so the weeks indoors come on top. Some packets say which. Many don't.

Set it against the length of your growing season. A very long-season winter squash in a short northern summer is a gamble, while a faster variety of the same squash isn't. It's also the number to use in midsummer when you're working out whether there's time for one more sowing.

## Depth, spacing and timing

Sowing depth is given in inches or centimetres. If it's missing, a rule that works for most seed is to cover it about two to three times its own width. Some seeds, lettuce among them, germinate better with light, so the packet may tell you to barely cover them.

Spacing often comes as two numbers, between plants and between rows. The plant figure is the one the crop cares about. The row figure assumes paths, and in a raised bed you can usually ignore it and use the plant spacing in every direction. If the packet tells you to thin, its spacing is the final one after thinning.

Timing will be written either as weeks before or after the last frost, or as a list of months. The weeks version works anywhere once you know your own frost date. The month version only really works where the packet was written.

## Germination temperature

Not every packet lists it, but the good ones do: the soil temperature the seed needs to sprout, and how many days that usually takes. It explains a lot of failures. Peppers and melons sown into cold soil sit there doing nothing and often rot. Peas and spinach will come up in cool soil. If the packet says seedlings appear in seven to fourteen days and you're on day twenty, the soil was probably too cold or too wet.

## F1, open-pollinated, and the small print

F1 means a first-generation hybrid, a cross between two parent lines. F1 varieties are often vigorous and very even, but seed saved from them won't grow true, and the next generation comes up mixed. Open-pollinated varieties, heirlooms included, grow true from saved seed as long as they haven't crossed with a neighbour.

Then find the date. Most packets carry a packed-for year or a sell-by date. Seed loses vigour with age, and some kinds fade much faster than others. Onion and parsnip seed is often poor after a year or two, while tomato seed kept cool and dry can last for several years. An older packet is still worth sowing, just sow it thicker, or test ten seeds on a damp paper towel first and count how many sprout.

Last, look for the word treated. Treated seed has been coated with a fungicide, the packet has to say so, and it's often dyed a bright colour so you can tell at a glance. Wash your hands after handling it, keep it away from children and pets, and if you garden organically, pick the untreated version.

**Pro tip.** Read the back of the packet in winter while you're ordering, not in spring at the bed. Days to maturity against your season length rules a variety in or out before you've spent anything.
