By late October the vegetable garden looks like the end of a party. Blackened tomato vines, a few heroic chard plants, beans gone to crackly brown pods. It's tempting either to leave all of it until spring or to rip everything out until the beds are bare and tidy.

Both go a bit wrong. The useful version is somewhere in between, and most of it is about what you leave behind.

## Start with the sick plants

Anything that ended the season diseased comes out first and goes in the trash. Tomatoes with blight, squash covered in powdery mildew, beans with rust on the leaves. Home compost heaps rarely get hot enough all the way through to kill disease, and you'll be spreading the problem back over the beds next spring.

The same goes for anything that was riddled with pests you don't want to meet again. It feels wasteful to bag good organic matter, and I still hesitate every year, but one season of blight that came back from my own compost was enough.

Everything healthy can go on the heap. Chop thick stems like squash vines and sunflower stalks into short lengths first, or they'll still be sitting there in a year.

## Leave the roots and cover the soil

For healthy annual crops, cut the stems off at the soil instead of pulling the whole plant. The roots stay in the ground and rot where they are, which feeds the soil life and leaves little channels behind for water and air. You don't disturb the soil and you don't bring up a fresh batch of weed seeds.

The exception is perennial weeds. Bindweed, dock and anything with a running root should come out properly, roots and all, while the soil is still soft enough to fork.

Then cover the bed. Bare soil over winter gets pounded flat by rain and loses its top layer to runoff. An inch or two of compost spread on top is a good start, and worms pull it down into the soil over winter. Over that, or instead of it, put shredded leaves, straw, or a cover crop if it's not too late in your area to sow one.

## A little mess is fine

You don't have to clear every stem. Seed heads on sunflowers and coneflowers feed birds into winter, and hollow stems give overwintering insects somewhere to sit out the cold. A corner of the garden left a bit scruffy until spring does more good than harm, as long as nothing in it was diseased.

## Two jobs that are easy to forget

Write down what grew where. It seems obvious now, standing in front of the beds, and it will be completely gone from your head by March. A rough sketch with crop names is enough to plan next year's rotation and keep tomatoes and potatoes out of the beds they were in this year.

And deal with the hoses before the first hard freeze. Unhook them from the spigot, drain them out, and coil them somewhere out of the weather. A hose left full of water on a frozen night can split, and a spigot with a hose still attached can freeze and crack back inside the wall. That one is a plumber's bill, so it's worth ten minutes this weekend.

**Pro tip.** Cut peas and beans off at the soil and leave their roots in the bed. They break down where they are, and the soil keeps everything they put into it.
