The usual advice is that sets are the easy way to grow onions and seed is for
people who enjoy fiddling. The first half is true. Sets are easy. You push them
into the soil in spring and most of them grow.

The second half is where it goes wrong, because seed isn't fiddly so much as
early. Onions from seed are about as simple as anything you'll start indoors,
they're cheaper, and you get to choose from far more varieties. What they ask
is that you sow them in the middle of winter, ten to twelve weeks before your
last frost. For a lot of the country that means January, and the decision has
to be made now, while there's still good seed to buy.

## What sets are, and why they're easy

An onion set is a small bulb grown from seed the summer before, lifted while
it's still tiny and stored over winter. When you plant it you're starting
partway through the plant's life rather than at the beginning, which is why
sets are so forgiving. They're past the fragile seedling stage before they
ever reach you.

That head start is the whole appeal. No trays, no lights, no thin seedlings to
keep alive through February. You plant them in early spring, a few weeks
before your last frost, because onions handle cold well, and you get on with
the rest of the garden.

## Where sets let you down

Sets bolt more readily than onions grown from seed. Onions normally flower in
their second year, and a set has already done part of its growing, so a cold
snap after planting can persuade it that winter has been and gone and it's
time to send up a flower stalk. A bolted onion makes a hard central stem and
doesn't store. Bigger sets are more prone to it than small ones, so pick the
small ones if you're choosing from a bin. Some suppliers, especially in the UK,
sell heat-treated sets that are less inclined to bolt.

The other limit is choice. Sets come in a handful of varieties, and at a lot of
garden centers they're sold simply as yellow, red or white with no variety
name at all. That matters more with onions than with most crops, for the
reason below.

## Day length decides which onion you can grow

Onions start forming a bulb when the days reach a certain length, and
different varieties respond to different lengths. Long-day onions need the
long summer days of northern latitudes, which covers roughly the northern half
of the US and all of the UK. Short-day onions bulb on shorter days and suit the
South, where a long-day type would never get the signal. Intermediate or
day-neutral types sit in between.

Plant the wrong type and you get either a plant that never really bulbs or one
that bulbs too early and stays small. With a named seed variety you know which
type you've got. With a bag of unnamed sets you often don't.

If you're not sure which group your garden falls in, a local seed supplier or
your state's extension service will tell you. It's the one question in this
whole decision with a right answer for your address.

## Why seed is worth the early start

Seed opens up everything sets don't. The long-keeping storage onions that last
into spring, sweet onions, flat Italian types, specific reds. All of them are
easy to find as seed and hard or impossible to find as sets. A single packet
also costs less than a bag of sets and will plant a lot more onions.

The work is less than it sounds. Sow thinly in a tray or a few seeds to a
module, keep them at ordinary room temperature until they come up, then give
them as much light as you can. They look like grass for weeks, and they're
supposed to. If they flop, trim the tops back to a few inches with scissors. It keeps them
sturdy and easier to handle at planting time. They go out a few weeks
before your last frost, the same time as sets would.

The ten to twelve weeks matter, though. A bulb's size depends on how much leaf
the plant has made before the day length tells it to start bulbing, so an
onion sown late goes into that switch small and stays small.
[How to grow onions big enough to bother storing](/blog/how-to-grow-onions/)
goes into that in more detail.

I'll admit my first attempt at onions from seed was a tray on a cold windowsill
in February, a good month late. They grew. They made onions about the size of
a golf ball, which was a fair lesson in what the weeks are for.

## The third option most people forget

You can also buy onion plants: young transplants, sold in bundles and shipped
or stocked at planting time. They're grown from seed by someone else over the
winter, so you get named varieties without running lights in January. They
cost more than seed and usually more than sets, and you're limited to what the
grower raised.

They're a good answer if you want a particular storage or sweet onion and
don't want to start seeds. The usual caution applies: check the label for
long-day or short-day, because bundles get shipped all over the country.

## So which one

If you want onions with the least fuss and you're happy with whatever's in the
bin, plant sets. Pick small ones and accept that a few might bolt.

If you want onions that store into spring, or a particular variety, or simply
more onions for the money, grow them from seed and put the sowing date on your
calendar now. Count back ten to twelve weeks from your average last frost. For
plenty of northern gardens that lands in January; further south it can be
earlier. [Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) works
that date out from your own frost dates if you'd rather not count weeks.

And order the seed in December. Onion seed doesn't keep well, so last year's
leftover packet is a poor bet, and the good varieties tend to sell out before
the new year. You can always buy sets in spring if January gets away from you.
You can't buy back the ten weeks.
