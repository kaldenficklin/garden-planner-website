Late October is when the bulb bags show up in a heap by the garage door, and the temptation is to plant them all the first warm Saturday. Tulips would rather you waited a little.

They want cold soil. Planted into ground that is still warm from September, they can start growing too early and they are more open to rot and disease. Planted after a frost or two, they sit quietly, put down roots, and wait for spring.

## When the timing is right

Use your own weather rather than the date on the bag. The window opens once you've had the first light frosts and the soil feels cold to your hand a few inches down. It closes when the ground freezes hard enough that you can't dig. For a lot of gardens that is a stretch of several weeks, and tulips are forgiving about where in it they land. I have planted them in December on a mild afternoon and had them flower perfectly well.

Daffodils are the exception worth knowing. They like a longer run at rooting, so put those in first and leave the tulips until last.

If you live somewhere winters barely chill, tulips may not get the long cold spell they need to flower properly. That is what the fridge trick in the tip above is for, and many gardeners in warm areas treat tulips as a one-season flower and replant each fall.

## Depth, and which way up

The rule of thumb is a hole about three times as deep as the bulb is tall. For a normal tulip that lands somewhere around six to eight inches. Going a little deeper is usually better than too shallow, because shallow tulips are easier for animals to reach and tend to flop.

The pointy end goes up. The flat end, sometimes with a few dry roots still on it, goes down. If you honestly can't tell (some bulbs are lopsided), lay it on its side. The shoot will turn and find its way up.

Groups look better than rows. Dig one wide hole, set five or seven bulbs in it about two bulb widths apart, and backfill. A ring of tulips around a bed edge is easy to plant, but a fat clump is what you notice from the kitchen window.

## Drainage matters more than feeding

Tulips sit in the ground all winter, and what kills them there is wet. A spot where puddles stand after rain will rot them. If your soil is heavy clay, plant them in a raised bed or work some grit into the bottom of the hole.

You don't need fertilizer for the first spring. The flower is already packed inside the bulb, which is why a firm, heavy bulb matters more than anything you add to the hole. Water them in once so the soil settles around them, and leave the rest to the weather.

## The squirrel problem

Squirrels watch you plant. I'm half joking, but freshly dug soil is an advertisement, and a row of tulips can be gone by the next morning. Lay a sheet of chicken wire flat over the planted area, pin it down at the corners, and cover it with a little mulch so it doesn't show. The shoots grow through the holes in spring. Lift it before they get tall, or the leaves get tangled.

Daffodils are the other answer. They're poisonous, and squirrels leave them alone, so if tulips keep disappearing, a bed of daffodils might be the easier fight.

**Pro tip.** If your winters barely get cold, chill tulip bulbs in the fridge for several weeks before planting. Keep them away from apples and other ripening fruit, which give off a gas that can spoil the flower inside.
