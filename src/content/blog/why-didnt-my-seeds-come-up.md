You sowed a tray, labelled it, checked it every morning, and two weeks on it's
still a tray of compost. Maybe a couple of cells came up and the rest didn't.
Maybe nothing did.

I'm writing this in November on purpose. The first indoor sowings of the year,
onions and leeks and the slow peppers, go in around January for a lot of us, and
that's when this happens most. The house is cold, the windowsill is colder, and
you're sowing seed you bought last spring.

The good news is that seeds that didn't come up usually leave evidence. Before you
resow, take a teaspoon and dig up a few. What you find tells you most of what went
wrong.

## First, are you sure it's been long enough?

Some seeds are just slow, and slower still in cool compost. Lettuce, radishes
and brassicas often show in under a week. Tomatoes take about a week in a warm
room. Peppers can take two weeks or more, especially if the compost is on the
cool side, and parsley has a reputation for taking three weeks or longer. Onions
are usually up in one to two.

If you're inside that window, wait. If you're well past it, dig.

## What the seed tells you

| What you find | What it most likely means |
| --- | --- |
| Seed looks exactly as it went in, firm, not swollen | too cold, too old, or not long enough yet |
| Seed has swollen and split, with a pale shoot curled underground | sown too deep, or a crust on top it couldn't break |
| Seed is soft, brown, mushy, maybe smells bad | rotted: too wet and too cool, or damping off |
| A tiny root came out, then went brown and shrivelled | dried out after it started |
| No seed at all, or empty husks, little holes | something ate it |

## Too deep

The usual rule is to cover a seed to about two or three times its own
thickness. That's barely anything for small seeds. Lettuce, many flowers and
some herbs want only a dusting of compost, or none, because they need some light
to germinate. Big seeds like squash and beans can go an inch or so down.

If you dig and find a seed that has germinated with a pale, crooked shoot that
never reached the surface, it ran out of energy before it got there. Also check
the surface of the tray. Compost that has been watered from above and then dried
can form a hard skin that small seedlings can't push through.

## Too cold, or too warm

Temperature is behind more January failures than anything else. Every crop has
a range where it germinates quickly, and a lot of the summer crops want their
compost warmer than most rooms are. Peppers and tomatoes do best in properly warm
compost. On a cold windowsill they'll sit for weeks, and some will rot before
they move.

Remember it's the compost temperature that counts, not the room's. A windowsill
can be several degrees colder than the middle of the room, especially at night
behind the glass. A heat mat under the tray, or a spot on top of the fridge for
the days before they come up, makes a big difference for peppers.

The opposite happens too. Lettuce and spinach germinate badly in warm compost and
can simply refuse. If you've put them on the same heat mat as the peppers,
that's probably your answer.

## Dried out, or too wet

A seed that has taken up water and started to grow can't stop and wait. If the
compost dries out at that stage, even once, it dies. That's the shrivelled seed
with a short dead root. Covering the tray with a lid or a sheet of plastic until
the first shoots appear stops this, as long as you take the cover off once they do.

Too much water is the other way round. Compost that stays soggy and cold has no
air in it, and seeds sitting in it rot. Fungi in the compost can kill a seedling
before it ever reaches the surface, too. That's damping off before emergence,
the same family of problems that makes
[seedlings fall over at the soil line](/blog/why-are-my-seedlings-falling-over/)
once they're up. If you find soft, brown, rotted seeds, use fresh seed-starting
mix and a clean tray for the resow, and water from below so the surface doesn't
stay wet.

## Old seed

If the seed looks untouched after plenty of time at the right temperature, and
the compost was moist but not wet, the seed may simply have been dead. Onion
and parsnip seed are the quickest to lose their viability, and onion seed is
often what people sow first in January. A lot of other seed keeps for years, so
don't bin the whole box on the strength of one failure. The
[ten-seed paper towel test](/blog/everyone-tells-you-to-throw-out-old-seed-most-of-it-is-still-fine/)
tells you in a week whether a packet is still worth sowing.

## Something ate them

Indoors, this is usually mice, especially if the trays are in a garage, a shed or
a cold back room. They'll dig up large seeds like peas, beans, squash and corn
overnight and leave neat little holes and the odd husk. Outdoors, birds take seed
from the surface and from shallow drills, and mice dig up the big ones.

Small animals tend to leave something behind. Little pits in the compost, scattered
husks, or droppings near the tray all point the same way. Move the trays somewhere
the mice can't reach, or cover outdoor rows with fleece or netting until the
seedlings are up.

## Before you resow

Change one thing based on what you found, then sow again. Don't change everything
and don't just repeat the same sowing in the same spot, hoping. Most seeds that
failed did so for a reason the next tray will share.

Timing helps with a lot of this. Sowing too early in a cold house is how peppers
end up sitting for three weeks. The planting calendar in
[Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) works out the
start-indoors date for each crop from your own frost dates, so the onions go in
when they should and the peppers wait until the house is a bit kinder to them.

And write the sowing date on the label, every time. The next time you're staring
at a bare tray, you'll know exactly how long it's been, which is the first thing
you need to know.
