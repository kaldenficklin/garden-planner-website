Microgreens are ordinary vegetable seedlings, cut and eaten when they are an inch or two tall. Radish tastes like radish. Pea shoots taste like peas.

They are the one thing worth growing in January on a windowsill in a flat, because they need no garden, no light rig, and no more than three weeks.

## What to grow, and the one safety rule

Start with radish, pea, sunflower, broccoli, mustard, kale and cress. They germinate fast and reliably, and radish in particular goes from seed to plate in about a week.

Buy seed by weight rather than in garden packets. You are sowing hundreds of seeds per tray, and a packet sized for a row of radishes will cover about a third of one. Seed sold for sprouting or microgreens is the same thing in a useful quantity.

The one hard rule is the nightshade family. Do not grow microgreens from tomato, pepper, eggplant or potato seed. The fruit of those plants is food and the foliage is not, and at the seedling stage you would be eating the foliage. Every other common vegetable and herb on the seed rack is fine.

Avoid seed sold as a garden crop that has been treated with fungicide, usually dyed pink or blue. That is meant to go in soil, not on a plate.

## Sowing, which is nothing like sowing a row

Use a shallow tray, an inch or so of damp seed compost, levelled and gently firmed. Any plastic food container with holes punched in the bottom works.

Then sow far more thickly than any instinct allows. Seeds almost touching, in a single even layer over the whole surface. There is no thinning, no spacing, and no competition problem, because nothing is going to grow long enough to need room.

Press the seed down into the mix rather than burying it, then sift the thinnest dusting of compost over the top. Water gently, ideally by standing the tray in a shallow dish for a few minutes and letting it drink from below, because a watering can will wash the seed into drifts.

Cover the tray for the first few days. A second tray upside down on top, or a plate, or a plastic bag. They germinate in the dark, the cover keeps the surface from drying, and the seedlings come up pale and straight and reaching. Lift the cover once the shoots are pushing against it.

## Light, water and about ten days

As soon as they are up, they need the brightest place you have. A south-facing windowsill in winter is enough for most of them, though they will lean hard toward it, so turn the tray every day. They green up within hours of getting light.

Water from below whenever the surface looks dry, and let the tray drain rather than sit in water. Damp is the point and wet is the failure: the one thing that goes wrong with microgreens is a fuzzy grey mould at soil level, which comes from a tray that is too wet, too crowded against a wall, or never gets any air movement. A cracked window for ten minutes a day fixes most of it.

They are ready when the first true leaves appear above the two seed leaves, usually somewhere between one and three weeks depending on what you sowed.

## Cutting, and keeping it going

Cut with scissors just above the soil line, taking what you need for that meal. Do not pull them up, and do not wash them until you are about to eat, since wet microgreens go slimy in the fridge within a day.

Most kinds do not regrow usefully after cutting. Pea shoots are the exception and will often give a second, smaller cut. For everything else, tip the spent tray into the compost and start again with fresh mix rather than reusing it, because the old root mat holds exactly the damp that grows mould.

The habit that makes this worth doing is sowing a new tray every week, on the same day, whether or not you feel like it. Three trays on a rotation is a continuous supply of something green through the months when there is nothing at all in the garden.

**Pro tip.** Never grow microgreens from tomato, pepper, eggplant or potato seed. The rest of the plant is edible later, but the seedling leaves of the nightshade family are not safe to eat.
