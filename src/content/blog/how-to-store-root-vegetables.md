There's a moment in late fall when the carrot row is the only green thing left in the bed, and you start wondering how long you can get away with leaving it there. The answer depends on the root and on how hard your ground freezes.

Root vegetables want two things in storage that most houses don't have: cold, close to freezing, and damp air. Everything below is a way of faking one or both.

## Leaving them where they are

Carrots and parsnips are the easiest to store because you don't have to move them. Before the ground freezes, pile a thick layer of straw or shredded leaves over the row, deep enough that the soil underneath doesn't lock up solid. Then dig them as you need them through the winter.

Parsnips are the ones that reward this most. Cold turns some of their starch into sugar, and a parsnip dug after a few frosts is sweeter than one dug in October.

Put a stake at each end of the row. Under a foot of snow and a layer of straw, a carrot bed looks like every other part of the garden, and I've spent more than one January morning probing for parsnips in the wrong place.

Beets and turnips are less happy left out. A light frost won't hurt them, but lift them before the hard freezes arrive. And if voles or mice are a problem in your garden, a mulched row is a winter buffet, which is a fair reason to lift everything.

## Getting them out of the ground in good shape

Dig on a dry day, with a fork pushed in beside the row rather than straight down on it. Lever the soil up and ease the roots out. Every stab mark and split is a place where rot starts in storage, so the gentle part matters.

Twist or cut the tops off right away, leaving a short stub of stalk. Leaves keep drawing water out of the root, and a carrot left with its greens on goes limp in days.

Then don't wash them. Let the soil dry and rub off what comes away easily. A thin film of dirt does no harm, and wet roots packed into a box are how you end up with a slimy one at the bottom.

Sort as you go. Anything forked, split, nicked by the fork or chewed by something goes in the kitchen pile to eat this week. Only perfect roots go into storage.

## Boxes of damp sand

The old way still works best for most of us. Take a wooden box or a plastic tub, put a layer of slightly damp sand in the bottom, lay roots on it so none of them touch, cover with more sand, and repeat. Damp sawdust or coir work too. The sand should feel like the inside of a sandcastle, not wet.

Put the box somewhere cold that doesn't freeze: an unheated garage, a cellar, a cool shed in a mild area. A heated basement is usually too warm, and the roots sprout or shrivel.

For a small harvest, the fridge crisper drawer does the same job. Bag the roots loosely so they don't dry out, and they'll keep for a good while.

## The check nobody wants to do

Every few weeks, dig through the box. One soft carrot left alone will turn the ones touching it, and by the time you smell it you have lost a layer.

It's ten minutes. Pull anything that feels soft or shows a dark wet patch, eat the ones going limp, and pack the sand back over the rest.

**Pro tip.** Keep stored carrots well away from apples and pears. Ripening fruit gives off ethylene gas, and carrots that sit near it turn bitter.
