Nearly every seed-starting date in the year hangs off one number: your average last spring frost. Know that, and a stack of seed packets turns into a calendar you can pin up in November and stop thinking about until it tells you to sow something.

It takes about half an hour, a pencil and a wall calendar.

## Find your date, and know what it means

Look up your average last frost date by zip code or postcode. Your local extension office will have it, and so will plenty of frost-date tools online. Write it at the top of the page.

Then remember what an average means. On that date there's still roughly an even chance of another frost coming after it. It's a planning number, and the weather is under no obligation to follow it. That's why the margin later on matters.

## Reading the packet

Most packets give sowing times relative to the last frost, in two kinds: start indoors so many weeks before, or sow outdoors so many weeks before or after. When a packet gives a range, like six to eight weeks, take the middle. When it gives a month instead, it was written for someone else's climate, so look up the weeks figure for that crop.

As rough guides, peppers often want eight to ten weeks indoors and onions from seed ten or more. Tomatoes are usually six to eight weeks. Broccoli and cabbage four to six. Squash, cucumbers and melons only three or four, because they grow fast and resent being held in pots.

## Count back and write it down

Mark your frost date on the calendar. For each crop, count back the weeks and write it on that day: tomatoes, sow indoors. Do the same for the outdoor sowings and the planting-out dates. You'll end up with something written on almost every week from late winter onward. Garden Planner Pro's frost-date calendar will do this counting for you if you'd rather not, but a pencil works fine.

Cool-season crops shift things earlier. Broccoli, cabbage, lettuce and onions handle cold, so they get planted out a few weeks before the last frost, and peas are often sown straight into the ground around then. That pushes their indoor start earlier still. Warm crops go the other way. Tomatoes, peppers, squash and beans go out after the frost date, once the nights have warmed up, and peppers in particular sulk if you rush them.

## The margin, and the mistake on the other side

I lost a whole tray of tomato plants one spring to a frost that came well after the date. Since then I plan tender crops to go out a week or two after the average, and I've never once regretted waiting. If your garden sits low, where cold air pools on still nights, add more.

Put hardening off on the calendar too. Seedlings need about a week of gradually longer spells outside before they can stay out, so that week has to finish on the planting-out date, not start on it.

And watch the other end. In my experience starting too early causes more trouble than starting late. Seedlings held in small pots for weeks past their time get leggy and rootbound, and they often end up behind plants sown later. If you're torn between two dates, take the later one.

**Pro tip.** The calendar tells you when you can plant out. The forecast tells you whether to. Check the next ten days before tender plants go in, whatever the date says.
