Most garden problems that get blamed on feeding are really about texture, drainage or pH. All three are cheap to find out and none of them change quickly, which is why autumn is the right time to look.

Three of the tests below cost nothing and take an afternoon.

## Texture, in two free tests

Texture is the proportion of sand, silt and clay, and it decides how your soil holds water, how fast it warms in spring, and how much it will let you walk on it in March.

The ribbon test takes two minutes. Take a walnut-sized lump of moist soil, work it between your palms until it is smooth, then squeeze it out between thumb and forefinger into a flat ribbon. Sandy soil will not hold together at all. Loam makes a short ribbon that breaks under an inch. Clay makes a long smooth ribbon and feels slippery rather than gritty.

The jar test is slower and more exact. Fill a straight-sided jar a third full of soil, top it with water and a drop of washing-up liquid, shake it hard, and leave it on a windowsill for a day. Sand drops out within a minute, silt over a few hours, clay over a day or more, and you get visible bands you can measure with a ruler.

Neither result is something you fix. Texture is what it is. What it tells you is which problems to expect and which amendments are worth the money.

## Drainage, which is the one that kills plants

Dig a hole about a foot deep and a foot across, fill it with water, and let it drain away completely. Then fill it again and time how long the second fill takes to empty.

The second fill is the one that counts, because by then the surrounding soil is already wet and you are measuring the real rate rather than dry soil soaking it up. Roughly speaking, an hour or two is good, most of a day is workable for a lot of crops, and more than a day is a drainage problem you have to design around rather than amend away.

Do this in the wettest part of the garden and the driest, because the answer often differs by more than people expect across one plot. If one bed is always the one that fails, this test usually explains it.

## pH, and why it matters more than feeding

pH governs whether the nutrients already in your soil are available to roots. At the wrong pH they are locked up, and adding more fertiliser does very little, which is the single most common reason a heavily fed bed still underperforms.

Most vegetables want somewhere between 6.0 and 7.0. Potatoes prefer it lower and are less prone to scab in acid soil; brassicas do better toward the top of that range and clubroot is worse in acid ground. Blueberries are the outlier and want it genuinely acid, which is why they usually belong in a pot of their own mix rather than in a bed.

A cheap kit from a garden centre is accurate enough for this decision. Test in several spots, and remember that lime raises pH slowly over months while sulphur lowers it even more slowly, so an autumn test is what lets you act before spring.

## The one test worth paying for

You cannot see nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium or organic matter with any home method, and the colour-change home kits for nutrients are not reliable enough to spend money on the back of.

A laboratory test through an extension service or a soil lab is inexpensive, comes back with actual numbers and a recommendation, and is worth doing once when you take on a garden and then every few years after. Follow the sampling instructions they send rather than the ones here, since labs differ.

If you do only one thing, do the drainage test. It is free, it takes a day of waiting and five minutes of work, and it explains more failed beds than any other single number.

**Pro tip.** Take one sample from six spots and mix them in a bucket before testing. A single trowelful from one corner tells you about that corner, which is almost never what you wanted to know.
