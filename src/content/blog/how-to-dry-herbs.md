By November the soft herbs have mostly given up, and what's left in the herb bed is the woody crowd: rosemary, sage, thyme, a bay tree that hasn't noticed the season change. Those happen to be the herbs that dry best, so one last cutting now fills a few jars for winter cooking.

Drying is easy and forgiving. Damp and sunlight are about the only ways to ruin it.

## Which herbs dry well, and which to freeze

The tough, oily-leaved herbs keep most of their flavour dried. Oregano, thyme, sage, rosemary and bay all dry well, and I'd rather have dried oregano than fresh in a long-cooked tomato sauce. Mint and dill dry reasonably well too.

Soft herbs are a letdown dried. Basil goes dark and tastes of hay, parsley loses most of what makes it parsley, and chives turn into something like lawn clippings. Freeze those instead. Chop them, pack them into an ice cube tray, top up with water and freeze. Once they're solid, tip the cubes into a bag and drop one into a soup or sauce while it cooks.

## Cutting and bunching

Cut on a dry day after any dew has burned off, so the leaves start dry. Take healthy stems and leave the plant enough to carry on. With rosemary and thyme, stay in the leafy growth and avoid cutting back into old bare wood, which often won't resprout.

Rinse only if they're gritty, then shake them off and leave them on a towel until there's no water left on the leaves. Tie the stems into small bunches, a thumb's width or so, with a rubber band. Stems shrink as they dry and slide out of a string knot, while a band tightens with them.

## Somewhere airy and out of the sun

Hang the bunches upside down from a hook, a coat hanger or a bit of string across a room, somewhere dry and airy and out of direct sun. Sunlight fades the colour and the flavour with it. A spare room works, or a dry shed, or the top of a cupboard away from the kettle. A paper bag with a few holes punched in it, slipped over each bunch, keeps dust off and catches leaves that drop.

The kitchen is often too steamy, and a damp garage in November can grow mould on a bunch before it finishes drying. Expect a week or two, longer in wet weather.

If you don't want to wait, strip the leaves onto a baking tray and put them in the oven on its lowest setting with the door propped open, checking often. A dehydrator on its lowest setting does the same job more gently. Either way, slow and low is the point. Herbs that toast lose the oils you're drying them for.

## Knowing it's done, and keeping it

Dry herbs crumble when you rub a leaf between your fingers, and the stems snap instead of bending. If anything still feels leathery, give it longer.

Strip the leaves from the stems and store them whole in airtight jars, somewhere dark and cool, away from the stove. Crush them as you cook, not before, because whole leaves hold their flavour much longer than crumbled ones. Write the herb and the date on each jar.

Dried herbs don't really go off so much as fade. By next autumn most jars will taste of less than they did, which works out, because that's about when there's a new crop to cut.

**Pro tip.** Check new jars for the first few days. Any mist on the inside of the glass means the herbs weren't quite dry. Spread them out for another day or two, then jar them again.
