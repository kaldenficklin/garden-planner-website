Not yet, if they're still green. And once they've gone completely yellow or
brown, yes, cutting them down and clearing them off the bed is usually the
better choice, though leaving them standing until late winter is a reasonable
plan too. What you shouldn't do is cut them while there's still green in them
because the bed looks untidy.

The green fern is what feeds the crown. All summer and into the fall it
photosynthesises and sends sugars down into the roots, and that store is what
pushes up next spring's spears. Cut it early and you've taken the last weeks of
that out of next year's harvest. Once the fronds have turned straw-coloured
right down to the base (in most gardens that means after a few hard frosts),
the plant has finished with them, and the question becomes what's best for the
bed over winter.

## Why cutting and clearing usually wins

The main argument for cutting is pests and disease, and it's a good one.

Asparagus beetles spend the winter as adults, tucked into sheltered spots in
and around the bed: old stalks, plant debris, loose mulch, anything with a
crevice in it. In spring they come out right as the first spears do, and the
spears they feed on and lay eggs on are the ones you're about to eat. Taking
the old ferns off the bed and away takes some of those winter hiding places
with them. It won't wipe the beetles out, because plenty overwinter in the
edges of the garden too. But it's one of the few things that cuts their
numbers before the season starts.

Two fungal diseases make the same case. Asparagus rust and purple spot both
survive the winter on the old fern stems, and their spores come off that debris
in spring to infect the new growth. If you saw orange or rust-coloured powder
on the ferns this summer, or small purple, sunken spots on the stems and
spears, clearing the old growth matters more. Bag it or burn it where that's
allowed. Don't put diseased ferns on the compost heap.

Then there are the berries. Female asparagus plants carry small red berries in
the fall, and the seeds drop and sprout into a carpet of thin seedling
asparagus that competes with the crowns you planted. Taking the ferns off
before the berries fall cuts most of that down. If your bed is mostly male
plants, which many modern varieties are bred to be, this won't matter much.

## The case for leaving them up

There are honest reasons to leave the dead ferns standing until late winter.

Standing stalks catch snow, and a blanket of snow is good insulation for the
crowns. In a cold, windy garden where the snow otherwise blows off the bed,
that can matter. The stalks also mark where the asparagus is. I put a fork
into a dormant crown one March, in a bed I'd cleared so thoroughly I'd
forgotten exactly where the row was, and I still feel bad about it.

And if this year's ferns were clean, with no rust, no spotting and not many
beetles over the summer, the disease argument is weaker. You'd be clearing
debris that isn't carrying much.

If you leave them, the one rule is to cut and clear them in late winter, before
any new spears show. That still takes the old stems and whatever's sheltering
in them off the bed before the beetles and the spores get going. Leave it until
the spears are up and you'll be snapping them off as you work.

## How I'd decide

If you had beetles or rust this year, cut and clear this fall, as soon as the
ferns are brown. That's the version I do most years, because asparagus beetles
are the pest I see most on my own bed.

If the bed was healthy and your winters are cold with snow that tends to blow
away, leaving the stalks up until late winter is fine, and you lose very
little.

If your winters are mild, there's not much reason to wait. Clean up in the
fall.

## Doing the cut

Cut the stems at ground level or an inch or two above it, with secateurs or a
sharp knife. Don't pull them, because you can tug at the crown. Rake up the
fallen fronds and the berries too. The fine needles get everywhere, and
leaving a mat of them on the bed undoes some of the point of clearing it.

Weed the bed while it's bare, since this is the only time of year you can see
the soil, and pull out any seedling asparagus while you're at it. Then spread
a couple of inches of compost or well-rotted manure over the row. In cold
areas a looser layer of straw or shredded leaves on top gives the crowns some
extra cover, which goes a little way toward what the standing stalks would have
done with snow.

Fusarium crown rot is the one serious asparagus problem none of this helps
with. It lives in the soil, not on the ferns, and a fall cleanup won't touch
it. If whole crowns are failing and the bed is thinning out year on year,
that's a different conversation, and it may be time to start a new bed
somewhere else.

Keep a note of what you did this fall and how many beetles you find next May.
A record like that, kept for a few years in something like the garden journal
in [Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278), tells you
more about your own bed than any general advice can, including this.
