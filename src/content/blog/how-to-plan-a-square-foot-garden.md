A square-foot garden is a raised bed divided into a grid of one-foot squares, with each square planted according to how much room one crop needs. Mel Bartholomew popularised it in the early 1980s, and the appeal is that you stop thinking about rows and spacing charts and start counting.

Now is the time to plan one. The bed is empty, the seed catalogues are turning up, and filling in a grid on paper is a fine way to spend a dark evening.

## The bed and the grid

The classic bed is four feet by four. The width is the part that matters: you can reach the middle from either side without stepping in, so the soil never gets trodden down and stays loose. Make it longer if you like, but keep it four feet wide or less. Fill it at least six inches deep with a light, rich mix, mostly compost with something to keep it open.

Lay a grid over the top with thin wooden laths or string, dividing the bed into sixteen squares a foot across. Leave the grid on all season. It's what keeps you honest on sowing day, when the temptation is always to squeeze in a few more.

## Counting plants per square

Each square takes one, four, nine or sixteen plants, depending on the size of the crop.

One per square is for the big ones: a staked tomato, a pepper, a broccoli, a cabbage. Four per square, set out in a little grid, suits leaf lettuce, chard, parsley, basil and marigolds. Nine per square works for bush beans, spinach and beets. Sixteen per square is for the skinny crops: carrots, radishes and onions.

The sprawlers don't fit the system well. A zucchini or a bush squash wants several squares to itself, and a vining squash is happier on a trellis or outside the bed altogether. Seed packets will give you wider spacings than these because they assume rows with paths between them. In a bed you never walk on, plants can sit closer.

## Tall plants go on the north side

In the US and UK the sun travels across the southern half of the sky, so anything tall on the south side of the bed shades everything behind it for much of the day. Put a trellis along the north edge and grow the climbers there: pole beans, peas, cucumbers, a tomato tied up rather than caged. Everything shorter goes in front, in full sun.

One year I put the tomatoes in the middle of the bed because that's where the gap was, and the lettuce behind them bolted in the shade in July. That was the last time.

## Planning the season square by square

The grid really pays off in midsummer. When you pull a square of radishes, you replant that square straight away with something else, a few beets or another lettuce, instead of leaving a gap. Some squares can carry two or three crops in one season.

So plan the bed in layers. Sketch the grid and fill in what goes in each square in spring, then pencil in what follows it in summer and what goes in for autumn. Mix fast crops with slow ones, so the bed isn't all ready the same week and then empty by July. Keep the plan, too. Next year you'll want the tomatoes in a different square, and you won't remember where they were.
