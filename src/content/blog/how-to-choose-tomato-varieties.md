Seed catalogues arrive in the dead of winter, and every tomato in them looks perfect. That's the trap. A big glossy beefsteak photographed in some long hot summer may never ripen in yours.

Choosing well comes down to a handful of questions, and the first one has nothing to do with the photo.

## Start with your season

Work out how many frost-free days you get between your last spring frost and your first autumn one. That's the window every tomato has to fit in.

Then look at days to maturity on the packet or in the catalogue. For tomatoes it's usually counted from transplanting, not from sowing, so add the six to eight weeks the plant spends indoors beforehand. And treat the number as a best case. A cool or cloudy summer slows everything down, and I've watched a late variety still hanging green on the vine the night the first frost came.

If your season is short, lean toward varieties sold as early, and leave the big late beefsteaks to gardeners with longer summers.

## Bush or vine

This is the other thing to settle before you fall for a picture.

Determinate tomatoes grow to a set size, flower at the tips, and ripen most of their crop over a few weeks before slowing right down. They stay compact, need only a short cage, and want little pruning. That makes them good in containers and handy if you're canning and want a lot at once.

Indeterminate tomatoes keep growing and keep fruiting until frost or disease stops them. They need a tall stake or a sturdy cage and some regular tying in, but they spread the harvest across the whole summer. Most of the big slicers and heirlooms are indeterminate. You'll also see semi-determinate varieties, which sit somewhere between.

## The letters after the name

Many catalogues print a string of capital letters after a variety's name. Those are disease resistance codes. The common ones: V is Verticillium wilt, F is Fusarium wilt (FF means resistance to two races of it), N is root-knot nematodes, and T or TMV is tobacco mosaic virus. Catalogues usually print a key for the rest.

Resistance means the plant copes better, not that it can't get the disease. It matters most if you've lost tomatoes to wilt before, or if you have to grow them in the same patch of ground year after year. Plenty of heirlooms carry no codes at all, and people grow them anyway for the flavour.

## Which kinds, and how many

Cherry tomatoes are the surest bet for a short season or a pot on a balcony. They usually ripen first, and a single plant keeps going for weeks. Paste tomatoes are meatier and less juicy, which is what you want for sauce. Slicers are for sandwiches and eating warm off the plant.

Then buy fewer plants than you're tempted to. A well-grown indeterminate tomato gets big, and a few of them crop more than most households expect. Mixing one early variety with one main-season one spreads the harvest further than doubling up on either. And write down which ones you liked in September, while you still remember. By January every name in the catalogue looks equally promising again.

**Pro tip.** Grow one variety you already trust and one new one each year. If the new one disappoints, you've still got tomatoes.
