Winter sowing is about the laziest way there is to raise a lot of plants. You sow seeds into a cut-open milk jug, tape it shut, put it outside in the cold, and mostly forget about it until spring.

The jug works like a tiny greenhouse. Rain and snow get in through the spout, the clear plastic lets light through, and the seeds come up when the weather tells them to. The seedlings never live on a windowsill, so they never need hardening off.

## Which seeds take to it

The best candidates are seeds that need a spell of cold and damp before they'll germinate. Many perennials work this way, including milkweed and coneflower. Sown indoors they often sit there sulking. Left outside in a jug over winter, they get the cold they need without you having to fake it in the fridge.

Hardy annuals that self-seed in your garden are also good bets, poppies among them. So are cool-season vegetables like kale, cabbage, lettuce and spinach, though those are better sown later in the winter.

Tender crops such as tomatoes, peppers and basil can't stand frost, and I'd leave them for the windowsill. Some people do winter sow them late in the season with decent results. I've had mixed luck.

## Making the jug

Any clear or translucent container with a bit of depth will do. Milk jugs are the classic because they're free. Rinse it out, then poke several drainage holes in the bottom. A jug that holds water rots seeds.

Cut around the jug just below the handle, leaving a strip under the handle uncut so the top folds back like a lid. Fill the bottom with 3 to 4 inches of moist potting mix (not garden soil, which packs down hard), sow at the depth on the packet, and water lightly.

Close the lid and tape all the way around the cut with duct tape. Leave the cap off. Label the jug with a paint pen or a label tucked inside, because ordinary marker fades in the sun by spring, and you won't remember which jug was which.

## Outside, and leave it

Set the jugs somewhere they get sun and rain and won't blow over. A group of them huddled against a wall works well. Then leave them. Snow on top is fine, and so is the mix freezing solid.

As for timing, seeds that need cold can go out in early winter, so they get the longest chill. Hardy vegetables are better sown towards late winter. Garden Planner Pro's frost-date calendar is a quick way to see how much winter you've got left.

On mild days, glance at the jugs. Condensation on the inside means the mix is damp. If the inside is clear and the mix looks pale and dry, water through the spout.

## Spring, and the part people rush

When the seedlings appear and the days start warming, open the lids on warm afternoons and close them again at night. A sealed jug in spring sun gets hot fast. Once the frosts are mostly past and the seedlings are pushing at the top, take the tape off and leave the lid open.

Plant them out in small clumps. Prising every seedling apart breaks a lot of roots, and a little clump planted as one, then thinned later, settles in better.

And if a jug shows nothing by mid-spring, don't throw it out. Some perennials are slow to germinate, and a few wait for a second winter.

**Pro tip.** Leave the cap off. The open spout lets rain and snow in and lets heat out on a sunny day, and a capped jug can overheat its seedlings by noon.
