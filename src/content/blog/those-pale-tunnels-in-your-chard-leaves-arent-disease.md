Go and hold one of the bad leaves up against the light before you read any
further. If the pale patch is a tunnel with a definite edge, a shape that
wanders and then widens into a blister, and the leaf between the two surfaces
feels papery and hollow under your thumb, you have leaf miners. There is a
larva in there right now, eating sideways between the top and bottom skin of
the leaf.

That matters mainly because of what it rules out. Everybody's first instinct
with a blotchy chard leaf is fungus, and half the internet will tell you to
reach for a spray. A miner is inside a sealed sandwich of leaf tissue. Nothing
you spray on the surface reaches it.

## How to be sure it is miners and not something else

The tell is the edge. A leaf-miner mine has a hard boundary, like something was
traced on the leaf, and the tunnel usually starts narrow and gets wider as the
larva grew. Hold it to the sun and you can often see the larva itself as a
small dark comma inside the blister, and a trail of dark specks behind it.

Downy mildew, which shows up on the same crops in the same damp autumn weather,
looks different: soft yellow patches on top with a grey or violet fuzz on the
underside when the morning is humid, and the edges are vague rather than drawn.
Cercospora leaf spot makes round tan spots with a distinct darker rim, scattered
all over rather than winding. Sunscald is bleached and dry and it only happens
on the leaves that were most exposed. None of those are hollow between the
surfaces. That hollow feeling under a thumbnail is worth more than any photo
comparison.

## Turn the good leaves over

This is the part that actually changes your season. The adult is a small grey
fly, and it lays its eggs on the *undersides* of leaves in a tidy little row of
white ovals, three or six or a dozen, set on end and lined up like seeds on a
ruler. Once you have seen one row you will spot them forever.

Go through the bed and turn leaves over. Every row you find and wipe off with a
thumb is a set of tunnels that never happens. This is the only stage of the
whole thing you can intervene in cheaply, and it takes about four minutes for a
short row of chard. I do it while I am out there picking anyway, which is the
only reason I keep doing it at all.

## What to do with the leaves that are already mined

Pick them. Not the whole plant, just the affected leaves, and either crush the
blister between finger and thumb before it goes in the compost or bin them
outright. A mined leaf is not going to recover, the larva finishes feeding and
drops out to pupate in the soil below, and every one you let complete that trip
is next spring's fly.

Chard and beet leaves regrow readily from the crown at this time of year, so
stripping a plant back to its smallest clean leaves in late September costs you
a couple of weeks and nothing else. And the mined part is only cosmetically
ruined, for what it is worth. If you cut around a small mine the rest of the
leaf is perfectly good food. I would not serve it to a guest, but I have eaten
around plenty of them.

## Stopping it next time, which is mostly a cover

The reliable control is a physical one. A light insect mesh or floating row
cover over chard, beet and spinach, put on at sowing and held down properly at
the edges, keeps the fly off entirely. It works because the pest arrives by air
and has nowhere else to go, and it is the only method here that does not depend
on you noticing anything.

Two things make covers fail. Putting them on a bed that grew the same crop last
year, when the pupae are already in the soil underneath, so you have fenced the
fly in rather than out. And leaving chenopod weeds around the edges, especially
lamb's quarters and fat hen, which are wild hosts and keep a population going
right beside your covered bed. Pull those when you pull anything else.

## The bit people get wrong in autumn

There is a temptation, at this point in the year, to decide it does not matter.
The chard looks rough, frost is coming, and who cares.

It matters because the last generation of the season is the one that overwinters
as pupae in the top inch or two of soil under the plants, and that is where next
May's outbreak comes from. Stripping the mined leaves off in late September and
lightly disturbing the soil surface under the plants before you mulch is a
genuinely cheap way to make next year quieter. Five minutes now against a month
of ruined spring spinach is a trade I will take every time.
