The bush beans are done. You pulled them out on a hot afternoon, raked the bed
flat, felt briefly organized about it, and now there is a tidy rectangle of bare
dirt sitting in the middle of the garden with nothing to do until April.

That bed should have something growing in it. Not another vegetable, necessarily
- a cover crop, sown thickly and never harvested, whose entire job is to hold
the soil together and then die into it.

I ignored this for years because it sounded like something a farmer does on
forty acres, not something worth the trouble on a four by eight raised bed. Then
I compared the bare beds in March to the one I had thrown oats into on a whim,
and I stopped arguing.

## What bare soil does all winter

Rain hits it directly. On a planted bed the drops break on leaves and trickle
down; on a bare one they hammer the surface, pack the top inch into a crust, and
carry the fine stuff into the path. By spring the bed you left smooth and fluffy
in September has settled into something you need a fork to open back up.

Weeds also notice an empty bed long before you do. Chickweed and bittercress
germinate in cool weather, sit green under the snow, and are setting seed by the
time you walk out in early spring with a plan.

And then there is the part nobody sees. The fungi and bacteria that make soil
behave like soil live on sugars that roots leak out while they grow. Bare ground
switches that off for six months. Living roots, even roots on a plant you never
eat, keep the whole thing running.

## The one decision that picks your cover crop for you

Everything else is detail. Ask this: **do you want the cover crop to die on its
own over winter, or survive until spring?**

If you want that bed ready early - peas in March, spinach as soon as the soil
works - you want something that winterkills. It grows through fall, freezes
solid, collapses into a straw mat, and in spring you rake it aside and plant.

If the bed is for tomatoes that go in mid-May anyway, you can afford something
that overwinters. It grows on both ends of the cold and puts down far more root.
It also means you have to kill it yourself in spring, which is real work.

With that settled, the shortlist is small:

- **Oats.** The one I recommend to anyone doing this for the first time. Fast,
  cheap, germinates in warm September soil, and in any climate with a genuine
  winter it dies on its own and leaves a blond mat of residue. Nearly impossible
  to regret.
- **Field peas or Austrian winter peas.** A legume, so it hosts the bacteria
  that pull nitrogen out of the air. Usually winterkills where cold is serious.
  Best sown mixed with oats, which give the vines something to lean on.
- **Cereal rye.** The toughest thing on this list. It germinates in cold soil, so
  it can be sown weeks after everything else has given up, and it will survive
  any winter you have. Enormous root system. You will have to cut it down in
  spring, and it does not go quietly.
- **Crimson clover.** A legume that overwinters where winters are mild, and a
  gamble where they are not. It needs a good six weeks of growth before hard
  frost to have a chance.
- **Hairy vetch.** The nitrogen champion, very winter-hardy, slow to do much in
  fall and then explosive in spring. It can also reseed itself into a nuisance.
  Sow it with rye and plan on being decisive in April.

## When to sow it

The rule is loose: give it about four weeks of real growth before your first
hard freeze, and closer to six if you are sowing a legume, because legumes are
slower out of the gate and want to be established before the cold arrives. Rye
is the exception at both ends and can go in a good deal later.

Which means the sowing window opens right about now in the north and stays open
into October further south. If you are not sure when your first frost lands,
that is the number the whole schedule hangs on, and Garden Pro Planner keeps a
planting calendar tuned to your local frost dates so you can count backward from
it instead of guessing.

Sowing takes ten minutes. Clear the spent crop, rake the surface loose, and
broadcast the seed by hand at a rate that will feel wrong - much thicker than
you would ever sow a vegetable, because you want a solid stand with no gaps for
weeds. Rake lightly so most of the seed is just covered, then water it in if the
weather is dry. That is the whole job.

## What happens in spring

The winterkilled ones handle themselves. Rake the residue off to the side or
leave it as mulch and plant straight through.

The survivors need cutting. Take them off at the soil line with a hedge shear or
a string trimmer before they set seed, when rye is roughly knee-high, and leave
the tops on the bed. Digging it all under is optional and mostly makes work.

One thing to know about rye specifically: its residue suppresses the germination
of small seeds for a couple of weeks after you cut it. Transplants are fine
going straight in. Carrots, lettuce, and anything else direct-sown and tiny
should wait two to three weeks after termination, or go in a different bed.

## Where I would skip it

Any bed you are planting garlic in this October. Do not cover crop that one -
the garlic is the cover crop, and it is going to be sitting there until July.

Beds you plan to sow with early carrots or salad greens, if cereal rye is your
only option locally. The waiting period fights the whole point of an early bed.
Oats there, or nothing.

And a bed you are going to bury under a heavy layer of leaves anyway. Leaves
cover the ground, feed the same organisms, and cost nothing. The cover crop is
better, but a leaf-covered bed in March looks nothing like a bare one, and doing
the easy version beats skipping it because the seed order never happened.
