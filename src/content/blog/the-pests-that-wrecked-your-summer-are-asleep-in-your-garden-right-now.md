Every summer I watch people fight the same pest for three months and then stop
fighting it in September because the plants are finished. That is roughly like
mopping up water for a season and never looking at the pipe.

The insects that took your squash apart in July did not migrate anywhere. Most
of them are within a few feet of where they were feeding, tucked into plant
debris or a couple of inches down in the soil, shut down for the winter and
waiting. Which means that an hour in the garden in October has more effect on
next July than anything you could have sprayed in July itself.

## Where they actually are

Squash bugs are the easiest to find and the most satisfying. The adults survive
the winter, and they do it under cover: dead vines left lying on the bed, a
board propped against the fence, the loose litter of leaves in the corner where
the beds meet. Lift a plank anywhere near where squash grew and you will often
find a cluster of flat grey-brown adults pressed together underneath. That is
next June's entire population, and you are looking at it.

Squash vine borer is down in the soil. When the larva has finished hollowing out
your zucchini stem it drops off and burrows an inch or two into the ground right
under the plant, spins a tough cocoon, and stays there until early summer. It
does not travel. The borer that killed a plant is overwintering in that bed.

Cabbage whites pupate above ground, as a hard chrysalis stuck to a stem, a
fence post, the underside of a raised bed frame. Slugs are laying clusters of
small pearly eggs in the top inch of soil right now, under anything damp and
flat. Colorado potato beetles and Mexican bean beetles dig in at the edges of
the beds and under leaf litter. Aphids mostly overwinter as eggs on woody
plants at the boundary rather than in the vegetable garden itself, which is why
aphids are the one on this list that cleanup barely touches.

## The winter is doing less of this for you than it used to

There is a comfortable assumption that cold sorts it out. Sometimes it does. A
long spell of genuinely hard frost, especially with no snow cover to insulate
the soil, kills a real proportion of the adults and pupae sitting in the top
couple of inches.

The trouble is that a mild winter kills very few of them, and a run of mild
winters is how a garden ends up with a pest problem that gets worse every year
for no obvious reason. If your squash bug numbers have been climbing season over
season while you have done nothing differently, the winters are the likely
reason. That is not something you can control, and it is a decent argument for
doing the part you can.

## What an October afternoon looks like

Start with the cucurbit beds, because that is where the return is highest. Pull
the dead vines and get them out of the garden rather than onto a heap at the end
of the bed, which is a shelter. Lift every board, pot, and tray that has been
lying on soil since August. The squash bugs will be under them, sluggish and
easy to deal with, and this is the one moment in the whole year when they are
not faster than you are.

Then, on any bed that grew squash, cucumbers or pumpkins, disturb the top few
inches of soil. Not a deep dig. A shallow turn with a fork, enough to break open
the borer cocoons and bring what is down there up onto the surface where the
cold and the birds get it. Do it on a dry day and leave it rough for a week
before you mulch.

Run your hand along the underside of the raised bed frames and the fence
palings while you are there, and scrape off the chrysalises. There are usually
more than you expect on the south-facing side.

## What to leave alone, because this can be overdone

The version of this advice that goes too far tells you to strip the garden to
bare soil, and that costs you something real. A lot of what eats your pests
overwinters in the same kind of mess: ground beetles under leaf litter,
lacewings and ladybirds in hollow stems and rough corners, solitary bees in dry
stalks. Clear the whole thing and you have removed both sides of the fight, and
the pests rebuild faster than the predators do.

So be specific rather than thorough. Take out the debris that was the diseased
or infested crop, in the beds where you had the problem. Leave the standing
seed heads, the rough corner behind the shed, the log pile, the perennial stems.
The difference between a garden cleanup that helps and one that backfires is
almost entirely whether you targeted it or just tidied.

If you only do one thing this month, lift the boards. It takes ten minutes,
you will know immediately whether you have a squash bug problem waiting for you,
and unlike almost everything else in the garden you get to see the result the
same afternoon.
