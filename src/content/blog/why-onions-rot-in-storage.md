The onion goes soft at the top first. You reach into the crate sometime in
November, pick one up, and the neck squashes between your fingers like a wet
paper towel while the rest of the bulb still looks fine. Cut it open and the
inner rings have gone tan and slimy from the shoulder down.

Nothing went wrong in November. That onion was lost in August, in the ten days
after it came out of the ground, and the whole business is decided by one part
of the plant that almost nobody watches.

## It happens at the neck

The neck is the pinched bit where the leaves meet the bulb, and until it dries
shut it is an open door into the middle of the onion. Grey mould spores are
already sitting on the foliage of a healthy crop, doing nothing much. They get
in through that wet neck, they grow slowly in the dark at storage temperatures,
and two or three months later you find the result. That is why the rot always
seems to start at the top, and why it shows up long after the harvest looked
like a success.

So curing is not a drying-out step you do to be thorough. It is the step that
closes the door.

Which also explains a thing that confuses people every year: the biggest,
proudest, thickest-necked onions in the bed are usually the first to go. A neck
the width of your thumb takes far longer to seal than a pencil-thin one, and
often never fully does. Bulbs that sent up a flower stalk are worse, because
that stalk leaves a hollow stem running straight into the center.

## Getting them out at the right moment

Wait for the tops to fall over on their own. When most of the bed has flopped
and the necks have gone soft an inch above the bulb, the plant has stopped
sizing up and is telling you it is finished. Stop watering at that point. Give
it a few dry days if the weather offers them, then lift the whole plant, leaves
and all.

Do not bend the tops over by hand to hurry it along. Old advice, still repeated,
and it bruises the neck at exactly the moment you need it intact.

Leave the leaves attached. They feed the last of themselves into the bulb while
it cures, and they are what seals that neck as they dry. Leave the roots on too.
And do not wash anything. Brush the loose dirt off once the bulbs are dry and
live with the rest of it. Water on a curing onion is the one thing you are
trying to avoid.

## Where they go for the next three weeks

Warm, shaded, and moving air. A covered porch, an open shed, a garage with a box
fan running, a spare room with the window cracked. Single layer if you have the
room, on something that breathes underneath, like a screen or a slatted crate.
Not a heap on concrete and not a closed plastic tote.

Shade matters more than people expect in August. Onions sunscald, and a scalded
patch on the shoulder is a soft spot waiting to happen.

Three weeks is typical and humid weather stretches it. You can stop guessing by
checking three things. The outer skins should rustle and slip a little when you
rub them. The roots should have gone wiry and stiff. And the neck should be
tight and papery all the way down when you roll it between finger and thumb.
That last one is the test that counts. Any give at all, any coolness, and the
bulb goes back for another week.

Then cut the tops to about an inch above the bulb, trim the roots close, and
sort.

## Sort hard, and eat the rejects first

Set aside anything with a thick or spongy neck, anything that bolted, anything
bruised or nicked by a fork, anything that has lost most of its outer skin. None
of those keep. They are good food this month and a liability in a crate in
December, so they go on the kitchen counter and get used now. One rotting onion
in a closed box takes several of its neighbors with it.

Variety decides most of the rest. Sweet onions carry a lot of water and little
of the sulfur chemistry that helps a bulb keep, so they measure their storage
life in weeks no matter how well you cure them. The pungent yellow storage types
are the ones that hold into late winter.

| Crop | Temperature | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Storage onions | 32-40 F | Dry, dark, air moving through. Mesh bag or open crate |
| Sweet onions | Refrigerate | A few weeks at best. Use them first |
| Garlic | 60-65 F | Dry and dark. Avoid 40-55 F, which wakes it up and starts sprouts |

Never in a sealed bag, never in the bin with the potatoes, and garlic does not
belong in the fridge, which is a common way a good crop gets quietly ruined in
September.

## Before you eat the best of the garlic

Your garlic came out in July and has been curing while the onions were still in
the ground. Same treatment: leaves on, shade, airflow, about three weeks, done
when the neck is dry and the outer wrappers are papery.

Go through it before it turns into dinner. The biggest bulbs with the fattest
cloves are next year's seed garlic, and clove size is most of what decides bulb
size in the crop that follows. Pull four or five of the best heads now and put
them somewhere you will not absentmindedly reach for them, because they go back
in the ground in October and you will not want to buy what you already grew.
Garden Pro Planner will hold that planting date against your local frost dates
so it does not slide past you in the middle of fall cleanup.

The rest is yours. Braid the softnecks if you like the look of them hanging in
the kitchen, though a mesh bag in a dark cupboard keeps them just as well and
takes ten seconds.
