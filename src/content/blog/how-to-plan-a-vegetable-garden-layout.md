A layout is not a drawing exercise. It is a set of decisions about reach, light and walking distance that you will live with every day from April to October, and most of them are hard to change once beds are built.

Two of them matter far more than the rest.

## Start with the sun, not the plan

Before anything else, spend a day noticing where the light actually falls. Not where you think it does. Go out mid morning, at midday, and at four in the afternoon, and note which parts of the plot are in sun each time.

Fruiting crops, which is tomatoes, peppers, squash, cucumbers, beans and anything else you eat the fruit of, want six hours minimum and do better with eight. Leaves are more forgiving: lettuce, spinach, chard and most herbs will take four or five hours and some of them prefer afternoon shade in summer.

Do this in the season you will be growing if you can, because a deciduous tree that casts no shadow in February throws a large one in June, and a plot surveyed in winter can be half shaded by the time it matters.

Then put the beds in the sunniest ground you have and accept the shape that gives you. A beautifully square layout in the wrong place loses to an awkward one in the right place every year.

## Four feet, and why the number is not arbitrary

The single most useful rule in bed design is that you should never need to step on the growing soil.

Soil that is walked on compacts, and compacted soil drains badly, warms slowly and grows shallow roots. Everything else you do, the compost, the mulch, the careful watering, is working against that if you are standing in the bed to reach the middle.

So beds are built to the width of two arms. Four feet lets most adults reach the centre from either side. If a bed is against a wall or a fence and can only be worked from one side, halve it: two and a half feet, maybe three.

Length is much freer, but long unbroken beds tempt people into walking across the middle rather than going round, so a gap every ten or twelve feet earns its space.

Paths need to be honest about what will travel down them. Eighteen inches works between beds for walking. Two feet or more is what you want on at least one route, because a wheelbarrow that does not fit is a wheelbarrow you stop using.

## Arranging what goes where

Put tall things where they will not shade short things. In the northern hemisphere that means corn, climbing beans, trellised cucumbers and staked tomatoes along the northern edge, stepping down to low crops at the south.

Group by how long a crop occupies its ground rather than by plant family. Radishes, lettuce, spinach and other fast crops in beds you will be turning over repeatedly; garlic, leeks, parsnips and winter brassicas, which sit for most of a year, somewhere you will not need to disturb.

Keep perennials out of the rotation entirely. Asparagus, rhubarb and permanent herbs want their own corner, because a bed you dig over every spring is no place for something that lives ten years.

And put the things you pick constantly nearest the door. Salad and herbs by the path get eaten; the same plants at the bottom of the garden get forgotten in August.

## Write it down before you forget it

Draw the finished layout on paper or in something that will still exist next January, with the beds numbered and what went in each one.

That record is what makes crop rotation possible at all. Nobody remembers in March which bed had the brassicas, and rotation done from memory is rotation done wrong.

It is also where to note what did not work: the bed that flooded, the corner that turned out to be shaded by two in the afternoon, the path that was too narrow. A layout is rarely right the first year, and the notes are how the second year is better.

**Pro tip.** Four feet is the number that matters. A bed wider than that cannot be reached from both sides, so you end up standing in it, and compacted soil undoes everything else on this list.
