You brought the pots in when the nights turned cold. The rosemary, the
geranium, a couple of herbs, the pepper you're trying to keep for next year.
Everything settled in fine. Then, a few weeks later, you notice tiny dark
flies drifting up out of the soil when you water, walking around on the rim
of the pot, and turning up on the window glass behind it.

These are almost certainly fungus gnats. They're a nuisance, they're very
common in pots indoors in winter, and on an established plant they do very
little real harm. You can get rid of them. It takes a few weeks, because the
part you can see isn't the part doing the breeding.

## Make sure that's what you've got

Three small flying things turn up around indoor plants, and they're easy to
mix up at first glance.

Fungus gnats are tiny, a couple of millimetres long, dark grey to black, with
long legs and long antennae. They look like very small, thin mosquitoes. They
fly weakly, in short hops, and spend most of their time on or just above the
soil. If you disturb the pot, they lift off the surface and settle again
nearby.

Fruit flies are tan or light brown, rounder and stockier, and if you get
close you'll see red eyes. They hang around the fruit bowl, the compost
caddy and the kitchen drain more than the plants. If the flies are worse
near the bananas than near the pots, they're fruit flies and the plants are
innocent.

Whitefly are white, not black, and they live on the plant rather than the
soil. Turn a leaf over and you'll see them sitting on the underside, and
when you brush the plant a little cloud of them flies up. They often come
indoors on plants that spent the summer outside, so check the undersides of
leaves on anything you brought in, especially hibiscus, peppers and tomatoes.

If what you're seeing is black, thin, and coming off the soil, read on.

## Why they showed up now

Fungus gnats lay their eggs in damp potting mix, and the larvae live in the
top inch or two of it. They're small, see-through little worms with shiny
black heads, and they feed on fungi and rotting organic matter in the soil.
Wet soil is the whole story.

Outdoors in summer, a pot dries out fast. Bring it inside in autumn and
everything slows down. There's less light, the plant grows more slowly and
uses less water, and the mix stays damp for days after you water. Most of
us carry on watering on the summer schedule anyway, out of habit. That's a
perfect gnat nursery. The eggs may have come in with the pot, or with a bag
of potting mix, or through an open window, and a few weeks of damp soil was
all they needed.

If you want to see the larvae for yourself, push a slice of raw potato
gently into the soil surface and lift it after a couple of days. Larvae
tend to collect underneath it.

## How to get rid of them

The order matters here, and the first step does most of the work.

Let the top inch of soil dry out between waterings. Push a finger in. If
it's damp at the first knuckle, don't water. Larvae can't survive in dry
surface mix, and adults don't lay eggs in it. Most indoor plants in winter
are happier drier anyway. Empty any water sitting in saucers or cachepots,
too, because a pot standing in water stays wet at the top.

If a plant really can't take drying out, water from the bottom instead. Sit
the pot in a dish of water for half an hour, let it soak up what it needs,
then lift it out. The surface stays drier that way.

Put up yellow sticky traps. These are small yellow cards on a stake that
you push into the soil, and the adult gnats are drawn to the colour and
stick. They won't clear an infestation by themselves, since the larvae are
still in the soil, but they catch adults before they lay more eggs and they
tell you how things are going. A card that's still clean after a week is
good news.

For a bad case, use a BTI product. BTI is *Bacillus thuringiensis* subsp.
*israelensis*, a soil bacterium that kills fly larvae (gnats and
mosquitoes) and is sold for organic use as granules, liquid, or the floating
"dunks" meant for ponds and water butts. You soak or dissolve it in water
according to the label and water the pots with it, and it works on the
larvae in the soil. It doesn't touch the adults, and it doesn't last long
in the mix, so plan on repeating it per the label for a few weeks while the
adults you already have die off.

I fought gnats for a whole winter once on a tray of overwintering herbs, and
it was entirely my own fault. I'd been watering them every Sunday like it
was still August. Spacing out the watering was what fixed it. The sticky
cards were mostly a way of watching it work.

## What not to bother with

Don't repot into fresh mix as your first move. It's a lot of disturbance
for a plant in low light, and if the watering doesn't change, the new mix
gets colonised just as fast. And don't reach for a houseplant insecticide
spray for the flying adults. You'd be treating the part of the problem that
lives a week or so, and leaving the part in the soil.

Gnats are worth taking seriously on seedlings and fresh cuttings, where the
larvae can damage tender roots. Keep any seed trays you start this winter
away from infested pots, and let their surface dry between waterings once
the seedlings are up.

Getting the watering right is the long-term fix. The watering
recommendations and
[reminders](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) in Garden Planner
Pro help with that, so the pots get water when they need it rather than
because it happens to be Sunday.

Give it three or four weeks. You'll see fewer and fewer gnats on the cards,
and one morning you'll water the pots and nothing will lift off the soil.
