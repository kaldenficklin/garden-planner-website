You brought the rosemary in before the first frost, put it somewhere sunny,
and watered it carefully. For a few weeks it looked fine. Now the needles at
the bottom are going brown and dropping when you brush past, and the tips look
dull and greyish. You've read that it's overwatering. You've also read that
it's underwatering. Both can't be right.

Both can be right, which is the frustrating part. But I've come to think they
are both symptoms, and that the thing deciding whether a potted rosemary
survives a winter indoors is where you put it. Get the spot right and the
watering becomes easy. Get it wrong and there's no watering schedule that
saves it.

## What a rosemary expects from winter

Rosemary is a shrub from dry, rocky hillsides with long sunny summers and cool,
bright winters. In a mild garden it stays outside all year, and plenty of
varieties will take a fair bit of frost if the soil drains well. What it gets
in winter there is lots of daylight, cool air, and a slow, steady growth rate.

Now look at the windowsill you put it on. Even a south-facing window gives far
less light than an open garden, because glass, overhangs and short days all
take their share. And the room is warm, often much warmer than the plant wants
in winter, with dry heated air on top.

Warm says grow. Dim says don't. The plant gets the two messages at once and
handles it badly.

## How the window turns into a watering problem

In low light, rosemary barely grows, and a plant that's barely growing barely
drinks. So the pot that dried out every few days on the patio in July stays
damp for a week or more on a windowsill in December. If you keep watering on
the summer schedule, the roots sit wet, and rosemary roots rot quickly in wet
soil. The first you see of it is browning needles, and by then a good part of
the root system has gone.

The opposite happens too, especially next to a radiator. The heat dries the
pot out faster than you'd expect from how little the plant is growing. And
rosemary has a cruel habit of not wilting. A basil plant flops when it's dry
and tells you. Rosemary looks exactly the same right up until the needles go
crisp, and once the root ball has dried out completely it often doesn't come
back.

So you get conflicting advice because people are describing two different
windowsills. The fix for both is the same.

## Find the coolest bright spot in the house

What a rosemary wants in winter is as much light as you can give it and as
little warmth. The best places I've found are an unheated porch, a cool
sunroom or a bright spare room with the radiator turned off. Anywhere that
stays above freezing but feels chilly to you is probably about right.

In a cool, bright spot the plant ticks over slowly. It uses water at a steady,
predictable rate, the needles stay green, and powdery mildew, which loves warm
still air, has a harder time getting going.

If the only bright window is in a warm room, add light rather than settling.
A cheap LED grow light a few inches above the plant for twelve hours or so a
day makes up for a lot of what the window lacks. I keep mine on a plug-in
timer so it isn't one more thing to remember.

## The watering, once the spot is right

Push a finger into the pot. When the top inch or so is dry, water thoroughly
until it runs out of the bottom, then empty the saucer. Don't water again
until the top inch is dry again. In a cool spot that might be every week or
ten days. In a warm one it might be every few days. Checking with a finger
works in both, where a fixed schedule only works in one.

A clay pot helps, because it breathes and dries out more evenly than plastic.
Make sure it has a drainage hole, and never let it sit in a saucer of water.

## The other two things to watch for

Look for a fine white dust on the needles, often starting low on the plant.
That's powdery mildew. Snip off the worst stems, give the plant more space
from its neighbours, and run a small fan nearby for an hour or two a day if
you can.

Look for fine webbing between the needles and tiny speckles on them. That's
spider mites, which thrive in dry heated air. Rinse the plant thoroughly in
the shower, undersides included, every few days for a couple of weeks.

## If the indoors just isn't working

Sometimes there's no cool bright room, and that's most houses. Two other
routes are worth knowing about.

If you're somewhere mild, the rosemary may do better left outside. Some
varieties, 'Arp' being the one most often mentioned, are noticeably hardier
than the common kind. Put the pot against a sheltered south-facing wall, out
of the wind, in free-draining mix, and keep the winter rain off it.

Or [take cuttings](/blog/take-cuttings-from-these-herbs-now-or-buy-them-all-again-in-may/).
A small rooted cutting adapts to a windowsill much better
than a big plant that spent the summer outside.

I still lose one now and then, and each time I've been able to trace it to
the plant being somewhere too warm and too dim. If you're keeping track of
your herbs, [Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) has
watering reminders, which help, as long as you still
push a finger into the pot before you pour. But move the pot first. Today, if
the needles are already dropping.
