The geraniums in the window boxes are often still flowering in late October, which makes it hard to believe one cold night will finish them. It will. The bedding geraniums most of us grow are pelargoniums from South Africa, and they have no frost tolerance at all.

The good news is that they're some of the easiest plants to keep from one year to the next. There are three ways to do it, and you can mix them.

## Keep the whole plant on a windowsill

This is the simplest if you have the room. Bring the pots in before the first frost, or dig plants out of the ground and pot them up. Check under the leaves before they come indoors, because aphids and whitefly come in with them and spread much faster in a warm house.

Cut them back by a third to a half and pull off dead leaves and old flowers. A smaller plant loses less water and fits on the sill.

Then give them the brightest window you have, in the coolest room you can. A warm, dim living room is the worst combination: they grow long, pale and floppy reaching for light. A sunny spare bedroom where you keep the heat low is ideal. Expect a few yellow leaves in the first couple of weeks while they adjust. That's normal.

## Water less than you think

Over winter, a geranium is barely growing, and wet compost in a cool room is how you lose one. Water only when the top of the compost has dried out, and let the pot drain fully. No feeding until spring.

If the stems near the base start to go black and soft, that's rot, and it nearly always means too much water. Take a cutting from a healthy tip right away, because the parent plant may not recover.

## Cuttings take less room

A tray of cuttings fits on a windowsill that couldn't hold one big plant, and young plants often flower better than old ones next summer anyway.

Take shoots from the tips, a few inches long, and cut just below a leaf joint. Pull off the lower leaves and any flower buds. Push them into small pots of gritty, free-draining mix and water once.

The usual cutting advice is to cover them with a plastic bag to keep them humid. Skip that here. Pelargoniums have fleshy stems that rot in humid air, so leave them uncovered in a bright spot. Most root within a few weeks, and you'll know because they start putting out new leaves.

## The paper bag method

The old way, from before everyone had sunny windows, was to store them dormant. Lift the plant, shake off the soil, and hang it upside down in a paper bag in a cool, dark place that doesn't freeze. Some people soak the roots now and then over winter. In spring you pot it up, cut it back to green wood, and wait.

I have mixed luck with this. Some years most come back; some years I lose half. If it's the only option you have, it's worth trying, but take a few cuttings as insurance.

Whichever way you go, the plants come out of winter leggy. In early spring, cut the long stems back, repot into fresh compost and start feeding again. They go back outside once your last frost has passed, after a week or so of hardening off.

**Pro tip.** No windowsill to spare? Lift the plant, shake off the soil and hang it upside down in a paper bag somewhere cool, dark and frost-free. Some come through and some don't, so take a few cuttings as well.
