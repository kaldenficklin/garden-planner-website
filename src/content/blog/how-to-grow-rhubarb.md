Rhubarb asks for one thing that most gardeners find genuinely difficult, and it is not a skill. It is patience. You plant a crown, it throws up a decent looking set of stalks in its first spring, and you have to leave every one of them alone.

Do that, and you have a plant that will feed you every spring for twenty years or more from the same spot. Pick it in year one and you get a handful of thin stalks and a weak plant that never really catches up.

## Plant it shallow and give it far more room than it needs

A rhubarb crown is a chunk of woody root with pink growth buds on top. The single most common planting mistake is burying it. Those buds want to sit right at the soil surface, just level with it or barely under. A crown planted two inches down will very often rot instead of shooting.

Dig a wide hole rather than a deep one, work plenty of compost or rotted manure into the bottom and the surrounding soil, then set the crown so the buds are looking at the sky and firm it in.

On spacing, take the label seriously. A mature plant is a good three feet across and will shade out anything you plant beside it. It is also permanent, so put it at the end of a bed or along an edge where you are not going to want to dig for the next decade or two.

## It is a hungry plant, and that is most of the care

Rhubarb is one of the few things in the garden you genuinely cannot overfeed. It is producing an enormous amount of leaf every year and it pulls that out of the soil.

Each autumn, once the leaves have died back, pile a thick layer of well rotted manure or compost over the whole area. Leave a gap of a few inches right around the crown itself, because material sitting directly on the buds holds water against them and encourages rot.

That is essentially the whole feeding programme. Water it in a long dry summer, especially in the first two years, and otherwise it looks after itself.

## Pull the stalks, and stop earlier than you want to

Take hold of a stalk low down, twist slightly, and pull it sideways and out. It should come away from the crown cleanly with a little white base attached. Cutting stalks with a knife leaves a stub that sits there and rots, and that rot can work its way down into the crown.

Never take more than about half the stalks at once, and stop harvesting altogether by midsummer. The leaves left standing through the second half of the year are what rebuild the root for next spring. Stripping a plant late is how people end up with a crown that produces less every year.

One thing that is not optional: the leaves are toxic and should not be eaten. Cut them off and put them on the compost heap, where they break down perfectly harmlessly.

## Flower stalks, and dividing an old crown

Sooner or later a thick round flower bud will appear in the middle of the plant, usually in a hot year or on an older crown. Cut it out at the base as soon as you spot it. Flowering and seeding takes energy the plant would otherwise put into stalks.

After five or six years the centre of the crown often goes woody and the stalks get thinner. That is the moment to lift the whole thing in winter, chop it into pieces with a spade so each piece has at least one fat bud, throw the tired middle away, and replant the outer sections. One old plant gives you three or four new ones.

**Pro tip.** In late winter, put an upturned bucket or a tall pot over one crown to block the light. The stalks stretch for it and come up pale, pink and tender weeks early. Only force a given crown every other year, because it takes a lot out of the plant.
