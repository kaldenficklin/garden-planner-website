Sweet peas are grown for scent and for the fact that picking them is the job, not a bonus. Neglect them for ten days in July and the whole row shuts down.

They are not difficult, but they are demanding in a specific way: deep roots, rich ground, plenty of water, and relentless picking.

## Deep pots, because of what the root does

A sweet pea puts down a long root very early, before it does much above ground. In a shallow tray that root hits the bottom, curls round and the plant is checked before it starts.

So sow into something deep and narrow: root trainers, cardboard tubes from kitchen paper, or tall pots. One or two seeds each. Modern seed rarely needs soaking or chipping, and soaking can rot it, so sow it dry unless the packet says otherwise.

You can sow in autumn or in early spring. Autumn sown plants overwintered in a cold frame or an unheated greenhouse make bigger root systems and flower several weeks earlier. Spring sowing is easier and still perfectly good, so start there if you have nowhere frost free but bright to keep them.

## Pinching, hardening, and being unkind on purpose

When a seedling has three or four pairs of leaves, pinch out the growing tip. One thin stem becomes several from the base, and several stems means several times the flowers.

Then treat them roughly. Sweet peas are hardy, and plants raised cold and given a proper hardening off are stocky and tough. Plants kept warm and cosy come out soft and pale and lie down in the first wind. A light frost does not hurt an autumn sown plant.

Plant them out into ground you have prepared properly. Sweet peas were traditionally grown in a trench filled with manure, which sounds like overkill and is not far off what they want: plenty of organic matter, holding plenty of water, in full sun.

## Support, and tying them in

They climb with tendrils, so they need something thin to grip. A wigwam of canes, netting stretched between posts, or twiggy hazel all work. What does not work is a smooth thick post, because the tendrils cannot get round it.

Young plants need help finding the support and tying in loosely for the first couple of feet. After that they take over.

How tall depends on what you want. Left alone they scramble to six feet or more and produce plenty of shortish stems. Growers after long straight stems for showing remove all the tendrils and side shoots and tie a single stem to a single cane, which is called cordon growing. For a garden, letting them do their own thing gives more flowers, just with shorter stems.

## Picking is the whole job

Every flower you leave on the plant will try to make a seed pod, and a plant with pods on it believes its year is finished. It stops producing new flower stems and the whole row winds down within a fortnight.

So pick every open flower, every two or three days, all summer. If you cannot use them, pick them anyway and give them away or compost them. Go over the plants at the same time and pull off any small green pods you missed, because those are what actually shut the plant down.

Keep the water up in hot weather, because dry roots also trigger the same shutdown and can make the plants drop their buds. A weekly soak at the base is far better than a daily sprinkle. A row that is picked and watered will run from early summer until the heat finally beats it.

**Pro tip.** Pick every flower every few days whether you want them indoors or not, and go over the plants for missed pods at the same time. A single swelling pod is enough to slow a plant right down.
