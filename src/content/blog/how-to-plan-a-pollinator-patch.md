Late autumn is a good time to plan this, with the beds bare and the seed catalogues coming. A pollinator patch doesn't need to be big. A strip along the end of the vegetable beds, or a corner by the gate, is plenty to make a difference you can see.

And it pays the vegetable garden back. Squash, cucumbers and strawberries all depend on insects to set fruit, and hoverflies, whose larvae eat aphids, come for the flowers too.

## Something in flower all season

The goal is a patch that's never completely without flowers from early spring to late autumn. Summer is easy. Almost anything blooms then. The gaps are at either end, and those are the times bees emerging in spring or feeding up in autumn have the least to go on.

For early in the year, spring bulbs like crocus, planted now, and early perennials. Chives flower in late spring. Summer can be borage, calendula, zinnias, cosmos and sunflowers. For the end of the season, asters, sedum and goldenrod keep going into autumn.

Write it out month by month while you're choosing. Where a month is empty, that's what to buy. Plants native to your area are a good place to start, since native bees are adapted to them.

## Clumps and simple flowers

Plant in groups rather than dotting single plants around. A clump of one kind is easier for insects to find, and a bee can work through it without flying all over the garden.

Choose flowers with simple, open faces where you can see the middle. Many heavily double flowers have extra petals where the pollen-bearing parts would be, so there's less on offer, or it's hard to reach. A single sunflower or a plain cosmos is more use than a ruffled pompom.

Mix up the shapes too. Flat clusters like dill and yarrow suit small flies and wasps. Tubular flowers suit long-tongued bees. Garden Planner Pro can help you place the patch alongside your vegetable beds so it fits the layout rather than taking a bed you need.

## Let the herbs go

Some of the best pollinator plants are already in the vegetable garden. Chives, oregano, thyme and mint are covered in bees when they flower. Dill and cilantro make flat umbrella-shaped flowers that small beneficial insects love.

So leave a few unpicked and let them bloom. You lose a bit of harvest from those plants and gain a lot of visitors. I leave a whole clump of oregano to flower every summer now, and on a warm afternoon it hums.

## Water, sprays and winter stems

Insects need to drink. A shallow dish with stones or marbles in it, so they can land without drowning, is all it takes. Keep it topped up in dry spells.

Keep pesticides off anything in flower. That includes some organic sprays, which can still harm bees. If you need to deal with a pest, pick it off by hand or spray only plants that aren't blooming.

And at the end of the season, leave some of the old stems standing. Some solitary bees and other insects shelter in hollow stems over winter. Cut them back in spring once the weather warms, rather than tidying everything away in autumn.

**Pro tip.** Next summer, watch the patch for ten minutes on a warm morning. Whatever is covered in insects, plant more of. Whatever they ignore, don't bother with again.
