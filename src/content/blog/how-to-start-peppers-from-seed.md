This one is written ahead. It's the end of November and nobody should be sowing peppers yet, but peppers are easy to start too late, and when they go wrong it is often at the very first step, in a cold tray where nothing comes up.

Now is when to order seed and find a heat mat, so that the sowing date in late winter doesn't arrive as a surprise.

## When to sow, counted backward

Peppers want a long run indoors before they go out. Sow them about eight to ten weeks before your average last frost date. Find that date for where you live, count back, and write it on the calendar now, because late winter is when it slips.

Hot peppers, especially the very hot ones, are slower to germinate and slower to grow, so they go in at the early end of that window. Sweet bell peppers can go at the later end.

Use fresh seed if you can. Pepper seed loses vigor faster than tomato seed, and an old packet that would still give you most of a row of tomatoes might give you a scattering of peppers, or nothing. A packet from this year or last year is the safe bet.

## Heat, and then patience

Peppers need warmth to germinate. Kept around 80°F (27°C), the mix gets them up in one to three weeks. On a cool windowsill they can sit for much longer than that, and some will rot in the cold damp mix before they ever try.

A seedling heat mat under the tray is the simplest answer. They're cheap and they make more difference to peppers than to almost anything else you'll start. Cover the tray with a clear lid to hold moisture, keep the mix damp without letting it get wet, and then wait.

The waiting is the hard part. I have dug up pepper seed after ten days, convinced the packet was dead, and found it swollen and just about to sprout. Give them three weeks before you decide anything, and longer for the very hot types.

## Light, and the slow middle stretch

The moment the seedlings are up, they need strong light. Take the lid off, turn the heat mat off or move the tray off it, and put them under a grow light hung close above the leaves. A windowsill in late winter is rarely bright enough, and pepper seedlings that stretch toward the glass end up thin and floppy.

When the first true leaves appear, the ones that look like small pepper leaves rather than the two smooth seed leaves, move each seedling into a pot a little bigger than the cell it's in. Don't jump straight to a huge pot. The mix stays wet around a small root system and the roots suffer for it.

After that, peppers grow slowly for weeks. That's normal. Water when the top of the mix feels dry, keep the light close, and leave them to it.

## Don't rush them outside

Your last frost date is the earliest a pepper can go out, and usually it's too early. Peppers hate cold nights. Even without a frost, a run of chilly nights stops them growing, and a stalled pepper can take weeks to start again. Wait until the nights are staying above about 50°F (10°C), and warmer is better.

Then harden them off over a week or so. Start with an hour or two outside in shade, out of the wind, and give them a little more sun and a little more time each day. Bring them in if a cold night is forecast. A pepper planted out a week or two late into warm soil usually overtakes one planted early into cold.

**Pro tip.** Bottom heat is what gets peppers going. A cold windowsill can leave the seed sitting in the mix for weeks, and some of it never comes up at all.
