A dahlia gives you more flowers per square foot, over a longer season, than almost anything else you can plant. From midsummer to the first frost it simply does not stop.

It is also a plant with three specific ways of going wrong: rot at the start, slugs in the middle, and cold at the end. None of them is difficult to handle once you know which one you are looking at.

## The tuber rots before it ever grows

A dahlia tuber is a store of energy with no roots attached. Put it in cold wet ground and it has no way to use the water around it, so it sits there and rots.

So wait until the frosts have finished and the soil has warmed. Plant four to six inches deep with the old stem or the growing eye pointing upwards, and then leave it completely alone. Do not water it in. Do not water it at all until you can see a shoot.

If you are impatient, start tubers in pots of just damp compost under cover a few weeks earlier and plant them out already growing. That also gets you flowering earlier, and it keeps the tuber out of the wettest ground at the worst time of year.

## Stake first, pinch early

Push the stake in before or at the moment you plant. A dahlia in full growth is heavy and brittle, and a cane driven in later, when the plant clearly needs it, goes straight through the tuber you are trying to support.

Then pinch. When the plant has three or four pairs of leaves, take out the central growing tip just above a leaf pair. As with zinnias, this converts one tall stem into a bushy plant with many more flowering stems.

Feeding follows the same pattern as tomatoes. Nitrogen early to build the plant, then a high potash feed from midsummer to push flowers. A dahlia fed too richly all season makes a magnificent green bush with disappointing numbers of blooms.

## Slugs are the real pest

Nothing eats a young dahlia shoot faster than slugs and snails. A shoot that has taken six weeks to appear can be gone overnight, and because the tuber has to start again from a lower bud, that costs you weeks.

The risk window is from the shoot breaking the surface until the plant is about a foot tall and toughening up. Check with a torch after dark, when they are actually out, and clear the hiding places at the base of the plant.

Earwigs are the other one, chewing ragged holes in the petals. They are far less serious, and an upturned pot stuffed with straw on top of a cane is the traditional trap: they climb in at dawn and you empty it out somewhere else.

## Cutting, and what to do in autumn

Cut dahlias generously and deep, the same way as zinnias, and deadhead everything you do not cut. A dahlia left to set seed slows down. It is worth learning the difference between a spent flower and a fat unopened bud, because they look similar from behind: the spent one is pointed and papery, the bud is round and firm.

When the frost blackens the foliage, you have a decision. In a mild garden with free draining soil, cut the stems down to a few inches and pile a thick mulch over the crown, and most tubers will come through the winter where they are.

In a cold or wet garden, lift them. Cut back, fork the clump out carefully, turn it upside down for a couple of weeks to drain the hollow stems, then store the tubers somewhere frost free and barely damp until spring. Checking the box once a month catches any that have started to rot before they take the others with them.

**Pro tip.** Do not water a newly planted tuber. There are no roots to take the water up, so it sits in wet soil and rots. Wait until you can see a shoot above ground, then start watering.
