Potatoes grow surprisingly well in a container, and tipping out a bag to find it full of new potatoes is one of the better moments of the year. You don't need a garden. A sunny balcony and a big enough pot will do.

The trade-off is that a container dries out fast, so the whole thing comes down to size and water.

## The container, and what to plant

Bigger is better. A grow bag or tub of at least 10 gallons (about 40 litres) is a sensible minimum for two or three seed potatoes. Fabric grow bags, old compost bins and big plastic tubs all work. Whatever you use needs drainage holes in the base, because waterlogged tubers rot.

Use certified seed potatoes rather than ones from the kitchen. They're sold free of the diseases that build up in potatoes, and supermarket potatoes are often treated to stop them sprouting.

First and second earlies suit containers best. They're quicker, they're happy in a smaller space, and they're usually out before blight gets going. Chit them first, in a cool bright place, so they go in with short green shoots already started.

## Planting and topping up

Put 4 to 6 inches of compost, or compost mixed with potting mix, in the bottom of the container. Set two or three tubers on it, shoots up, and cover them with a few more inches.

As the shoots grow, keep adding compost around them, leaving the top few leaves showing each time. Stop when you're near the rim. This does the same job as earthing up in the ground: it keeps the tubers covered and dark.

Plant around your last frost date. The leaves are tender, but a container has one big advantage here. If a late frost turns up in the forecast, you can drag it under cover for the night. Garden Planner Pro's frost-date calendar will tell you roughly when to start.

## Water, and more water

Potatoes in containers need full sun and steady watering. In a hot spell a bag can dry out in a day, so check it daily by pushing a finger into the compost. If it's dry a couple of inches down, water until it runs out of the bottom.

Even watering matters as well as enough of it. A pot that swings between bone dry and soaked tends to give you cracked or misshapen tubers. A general-purpose feed every couple of weeks once the plants are growing well helps too, since a container has only so much to give.

## Tipping it out

Early potatoes are usually ready around the time the plants flower, though some varieties hardly flower at all. Scrape away some compost at the top and have a look. If the potatoes are the size you like, they're ready.

For the full harvest, wait until the leaves start to yellow, then tip the whole container out onto a sheet or tarp and sift through it. You'll miss a few however careful you are.

Don't reuse that compost for potatoes or tomatoes next year. Spread it on a bed growing something else.

**Pro tip.** Keep every tuber covered. Potatoes that see light turn green, and green skin contains solanine, which you shouldn't eat.
