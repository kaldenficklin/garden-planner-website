Ginger is a tropical plant that needs most of a year of warmth to make a decent crop, which sounds hopeless anywhere with a real winter. It isn't. Start it indoors in late winter, keep it in a pot so it can come and go, and you'll dig your own in autumn.

It's also one of the easiest things to try, because the starting material might already be in your kitchen.

## Choosing a piece that will grow

Seed ginger from a plant supplier is the surest start. Supermarket ginger works too, but some of it is treated to stop it sprouting in the shop. Organic ginger tends to sprout more reliably, so that's the one to pick if you're buying from a grocery.

Look for a plump, firm piece with smooth skin, not shrivelled. You want eyes: small pale or yellowish bumps, often near the tips of the fingers. Those are the growth buds, and no eyes means no plant.

Soak the piece overnight in a bowl of plain water. A large piece can be broken into chunks with an eye or two on each. Leave the broken ends to dry for a day before planting so they're less likely to rot.

## Planting and the long wait

Ginger grows sideways rather than down, so choose a wide, fairly shallow pot over a deep one. Fill it with free-draining potting compost and lay the pieces on top with the eyes pointing up. Cover them with about an inch of compost.

Water once, then keep the compost barely damp until shoots appear. Wet cold compost and a dormant rhizome add up to rot.

Then be patient. Ginger needs warmth to sprout and it can take several weeks even in a warm room. I've nearly thrown out a pot that turned out to be fine and was just slow. A spot near a heat source, or on a heat mat, speeds it up.

## Summer

Once shoots are up, give the pot bright light and water more as the leaves grow. The leaves look a bit like bamboo, and by midsummer a happy plant is a nice thing to have on a patio.

Move it outside only when nights are reliably warm, which is well after your last frost. Ginger likes bright light but appreciates some shade from strong afternoon sun, and shelter from wind. Keep the compost evenly moist all summer and give it a general liquid feed now and then.

## Digging it up

As the nights cool in autumn, growth slows and the leaves start to yellow. That's your cue. Bring the pot in or dig it before the first frost, since cold damages it quickly.

Tip the pot out and you'll find the rhizome has spread across it. Young ginger has thin pink-tinged skin and a milder taste than shop ginger. If you can't wait, you can snap a small piece off the edge in late summer without lifting the whole plant.

Save a good piece with eyes on it. Kept cool and dry over winter, it's next year's start. Garden Planner Pro's frost-date calendar is a handy way to remember when to bring the pot in.

**Pro tip.** Warmth is what wakes ginger up. A pot on a cold windowsill can sit for weeks doing nothing, so if yours hasn't sprouted, move it somewhere warmer before you give up on it.
