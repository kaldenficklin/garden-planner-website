You went out to pull a few carrots for dinner and the first one came up with
a crack running down its side, top to nearly the tip, like someone had taken a
knife to it. Then the second one did too. They looked perfect from above.
Feathery tops, good shoulders showing at the soil line. And underneath, half
of them have split open.

They're fine to eat. A split carrot is a carrot that grew too fast for its own
skin, and there's nothing wrong with it except the crack. And if you've had a
dry spell followed by a week of autumn rain, that rain is almost certainly
what did it.

## Why carrots split

A carrot is mostly water. Through a dry stretch, the root slows down and its
outer layers firm up. Then the rain comes, the soil goes from dry to soaked
in a day or two, and the root takes up water fast. The inside swells faster
than the outside can stretch, and the skin gives way along its length.

It happens most to carrots that are already full size, which is why it shows
up now. Roots that have been sitting in the ground at maturity for a few
weeks waiting to be dug are the ones most likely to crack when the weather
turns wet. Some varieties are more prone to it than others, too. I lost most
of a row one wet October to a variety I'd never had trouble with in the
spring, and I still don't really know whether that was the variety or just
that year's rain.

## Is it a split, a fork, or something eating it?

New growers tend to lump every ugly carrot together, and they have different
causes. Lay the bad ones out on the path and look at them in daylight.

A split is one clean crack running lengthwise, usually deep, sometimes
gaping. If it happened in the last few days, the inside is bright and moist.
If it happened weeks ago, the edges will have dried and healed over with a
rougher, darker skin. Either way, there are no holes and no tunnels. The rest
of the carrot is firm.

A fork is a carrot with two or more legs, or one that bends sharply partway
down. That isn't the rain. It happened months ago, when the growing tip of
the young root hit something: a stone, a hard clod, a compacted layer, or
sometimes a patch of rich, fresh manure. Forked carrots taste the same as
straight ones; they're just a nuisance to peel. The fix is in the soil next
spring, and [the carrot growing guide](/blog/how-to-grow-carrots/) goes
through it.

Tunnels are a different matter. Thin, winding, rust-brown channels just under
the skin, often more of them toward the lower half of the root, mean carrot
rust fly (the same insect UK growers call carrot fly). If you snap a damaged
carrot you may find a small, creamy-white maggot inside. That's an insect
problem, and it's the one on this list that's worth doing something about.

Small round holes, straight and narrow, as if made with a thin skewer, are
usually wireworms. They're the shiny, stiff, orange-brown larvae of click
beetles, and they're common in beds that were lawn not long ago. Shallow,
scooped-out holes near the top of the root are more likely slugs.

## What to do today

Dig a few more from different parts of the row so you know what you're
dealing with, rather than judging the whole bed by the first two.

If they're split, bring the split ones in and eat them first. Cut out any
part of the crack that's gone soft or dark, and use the rest as you normally
would. Raw, roasted, in soup; nobody at the table will know. What split
carrots don't do is keep. The crack lets rot in, so don't put them in a
storage box with the good ones. Treat them as this week's carrots.

For the rest of the row, look at the forecast. If more heavy rain is on the
way and the carrots are already a good size, I'd dig them now and store them
somewhere cool rather than leave them to swell and crack. If you'd rather
keep them in the ground (they hold well there in a lot of places), a thick
layer of straw or shredded leaves over the row helps keep the soil moisture
more even and the ground from freezing hard later. There's more on that in
[the hard freeze post](/blog/every-way-to-protect-fall-crops-from-a-hard-freeze-ranked/).

If you found rust fly tunnels, the advice flips. Dig the whole row now,
don't leave it in for winter. The larvae keep feeding in roots left in the
ground, and the damage only gets worse the longer they sit. Cut away the
tunnelled parts and the rest of the carrot is perfectly edible, but these
won't store well either, so eat them over the next few weeks. Bag the
scraps rather than composting them next to the carrot bed. If what you dug
up had tunnels and you're not sure which insect made them, the pest guides
in Garden Planner Pro have treatment tips and can help you put a name to
it.

Wireworm-holed carrots are edible once you cut out the holes. They tend to
be worse the first year or two after turning lawn into a bed, and they ease
off as the bed gets worked.

## Next year's carrots

For splitting, the cure is even moisture. Carrots that get a steady supply of
water all season don't go through that dry-then-flooded swing, and a layer of
mulch between the rows once the seedlings are up helps a great deal. You can't
stop it raining in October, but if the soil was never bone dry beforehand, the
rain does far less damage. Dig carrots once they reach a good size instead of
leaving the whole row in as a store, at least in a wet autumn.

For rust fly, the reliable answer is a physical barrier: fine insect mesh or
row cover over the row from sowing day, edges sealed, so the adult flies
can't lay eggs at the base of the plants.

And if this is your first year growing carrots and half the row came up
cracked, that's an ordinary first-year carrot harvest. The split ones roast
exactly as well as the straight ones.
