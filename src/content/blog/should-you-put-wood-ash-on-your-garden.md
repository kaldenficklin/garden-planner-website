The first fires of the season have been lit, and by now there's a bucket of
grey ash by the back door with nowhere in particular to go. Someone has told you
it's good for the garden. Someone else has told you it'll ruin it.

Both of them are partly right. Wood ash can be good for a garden, in small
amounts, on soil that needs what it has. It works like a mild lime, so it raises
your soil's pH, and it carries a fair amount of potassium plus some calcium and
magnesium. That makes it useful on acidic soil and unhelpful on soil that's
already neutral or alkaline. So test your pH before any of it goes down, keep it
well away from blueberries and from wherever next year's potatoes are going, and
never use ash from charcoal briquettes.

## What's in the bucket

Burned wood leaves behind the minerals the tree pulled out of the ground. Most
of what's left is calcium in forms that neutralise acid, which is why ash behaves
like lime. There's potassium too, which is where the old word "potash" comes
from: people used to leach it out of wood ash in pots. There's a little
phosphorus and magnesium, and no nitrogen at all, since that goes up the chimney.

Ash from hardwoods like oak and maple generally carries more of all this than ash
from pine and other softwoods. The exact figures swing a lot with the wood and
how hot it burned, so I wouldn't put much faith in any precise analysis you see
quoted.

What matters for your garden is the lime part. Ash is a weaker liming material
than ground garden lime, but it acts faster, and it's very easy to overdo
because it's free and there's always more of it.

## When it helps

If a soil test comes back acidic, somewhere well under the 6.0 to 7.0 range most
vegetables are happiest in, ash is a reasonable way to nudge it up. Plenty of
gardens in high-rainfall, wooded country sit there naturally, and some bagged
composts and peat-heavy raised bed mixes start out on the acidic side too.

On soil like that, a light dusting worked into the top couple of inches does some
good. The potassium is a bonus for fruiting crops and root crops, and cabbages
and their relatives don't like soil that's too acidic.

How light is light? A common ceiling is roughly 10 to 15 pounds per 1,000 square
feet a year. For a 4-by-8 raised bed that comes out to something like a couple of
cupfuls a year. Not a bucket. Spread it thin on a still day, rake it in, and
don't come back with more until you've tested again.

I used to tip the whole winter's ash onto one bed every March, because it was
the bed closest to the house. That bed grew worse spinach every year for three
years before I bought a test kit and found out why.

## When it doesn't

If your soil is already at 7.0 or above, ash pushes it further toward alkaline,
and at high pH several nutrients (iron and manganese among them) get harder for
plants to take up. Chalky soils, much of the arid West, and any bed that's been
limed regularly don't need it. Test before you assume.

### Blueberries and other acid lovers

Blueberries want soil around 4.5 to 5.5, far more acidic than most vegetables
like, and getting a bed down there is slow work. A season of casual ash can undo
a lot of it. Keep ash away from [blueberries](/blog/how-to-grow-blueberries/),
and from rhododendrons, azaleas, and anything else you've gone to trouble to keep
acidic.

### Wherever the potatoes are going

Potato scab, the rough corky patches on the skins, comes from soil bacteria that
do better as pH rises. Slightly acidic soil keeps it in check, which is why
liming a bed right before [potatoes](/blog/how-to-grow-potatoes/) go in is a
reliable way to give yourself a scabby harvest. If you rotate your crops, find
out which bed the potatoes are moving into before the ash goes out. Laying next
year's beds out on Garden Planner Pro's square-foot grid over the winter gives
you that answer before you need it.

### Charcoal briquettes, painted wood, and the rest

Ash from barbecue briquettes is a different thing. Briquettes are made with
binders and other additives, and the quick-light kind carries lighter fluid.
That ash goes in the trash. So does ash from painted, stained or
pressure-treated lumber, from plywood, or from anything else you burned that
wasn't plain wood.

## A few cautions that catch people out

Ash is caustic when it's wet. Wear gloves to spread it and keep it out of your
eyes. Don't put it right around seedlings or on top of a row you've just sown,
and don't let it sit on leaves.

Don't spread it at the same time as a nitrogen fertiliser like ammonium sulfate
or urea, or with fresh manure. The alkaline ash can turn some of that nitrogen
into ammonia gas that drifts off before the plants get any. Leave a few weeks
between them.

Ash can hold live embers for days after the fire looks dead. Keep it in a metal
bucket with a lid, away from the house, the shed and the leaf pile, until it's
completely cold.

And the slug barrier trick, a ring of ash around a lettuce, works only while the
ash stays dry. One dewy night and it's a grey smear. I wouldn't bother.

## What to do with the rest of the winter's ash

Most of us burn far more wood than a garden can sensibly take. A thin sprinkle
between layers in the [compost heap](/blog/how-to-make-compost/) is fine. The
rest can go in the bin, or to a neighbour whose soil test came back sour.

If you do one thing this week, test the soil in the bed you were planning to
ash, while you can still get a trowel into it. That one number decides whether
the bucket by the back door is a soil amendment or just something to get rid of.
