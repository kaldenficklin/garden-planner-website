The advice you'll find first, if you search for purple leaves, is that it means
phosphorus deficiency and you should feed with bone meal or a high-phosphorus
fertiliser. In a summer seedling tray, that can be true. On kale, cabbage,
broccoli or lettuce in the garden at the end of September, it almost never is.

What you're looking at is the plant's reaction to cold nights. Put the bone
meal back on the shelf. The purple is harmless, it often means the leaves are
about to get sweeter, and adding fertiliser won't turn it back.

## Why cold turns leaves purple

Leaves are full of red and purple pigments called anthocyanins all year; most
of the time the green of the chlorophyll hides them. When nights get cold, a
lot of plants make more anthocyanin, partly as a sort of sunscreen for leaves
that can't use light as efficiently at low temperatures. So the purple shows
through, usually starting on the older, outer leaves and along the veins and
leaf edges.

The cabbage family does this readily, and so do red and bronze lettuces, which
can go from faintly tinted to deep burgundy in a single cold week. Kale is the
one that frightens people, because a green variety like a Scotch curled can
come out looking bruised. It isn't. I pulled a whole row of kale one October
because I'd convinced myself it was sick, and I still think about what I threw
away.

## Where the phosphorus idea comes from

The advice isn't invented. Phosphorus deficiency really does cause purpling,
on the older leaves first, and the textbook photos look a lot like a cold kale
plant.

The part people miss is that cold soil slows down how much
phosphorus a root can take up, even when there's plenty in the ground. So in
autumn the plant may be showing mild phosphorus stress without the soil being
short of anything. Adding more phosphorus doesn't help a root that is too cold
to take it in, and in a few weeks it won't be growing much anyway.

Bone meal is a particularly poor fix. It releases its phosphorus slowly, and
very little of it at all in alkaline soil. And home vegetable beds that have
had years of compost and manure often already carry more phosphorus than they
need. Extra phosphorus isn't free: what the plants don't use can wash out of
the bed with the winter rain.

## When purple does mean something

There are times it's worth taking seriously.

If it's summer, the nights are warm, and a young plant is purple and small and
not growing, that's a different story. A plant that is stunted as well as
coloured is the one to worry about. Same if the purple comes with dead, brown
or scorched edges, or if it's on the youngest leaves in the middle rather than
the old ones round the outside.

Tomato and pepper seedlings are the classic case in spring. Purple undersides
on seedlings that have been sitting in a cold room or a chilly cold frame
usually clear up once they're warmer. If they're warm and still purple and
still not growing, it's worth a soil test before you feed anything.

That's the answer to phosphorus questions generally, really. A basic soil test
tells you whether you're short. Guessing from leaf colour mostly tells you what
the weather did last week.

## What to do with purple fall greens

Keep picking. Nothing about the colour makes the leaves less edible, and the
same cold that brings the purple out also makes kale and cabbage taste better.
As temperatures drop, these plants convert some of their starch to sugar,
which acts a bit like antifreeze in the leaf. That's why kale picked after the
first frosts is so much sweeter than August kale.

Take the lowest, oldest leaves first, the way you would anyway. They're the
ones that colour up first and they're also the ones slugs hide under.

If you want to hold onto the harvest longer, a row cover or a cold frame will
keep the worst of the cold off. The plants will stay greener, and they'll also
be a little less sweet. That's a trade I'm happy to make in November, not in
October.

If you keep notes, it's worth writing down the week your greens first colour
up. The garden journal in [Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278)
keeps each season next to the last, so the next time a row of kale goes purple
you can see that it did the same thing, around the same week, the year before.

## Varieties that are supposed to be purple

A last check before you worry at all: some of it may be the seed. Red Russian
kale has purple stems and veins, Redbor is purple all over, and red cabbage and
most red lettuces are meant to be exactly that colour. Cold just makes them
deeper. If the packet is gone and you can't remember what you sowed, look at
the stems. Anything with purple stems in August was purple on purpose.
