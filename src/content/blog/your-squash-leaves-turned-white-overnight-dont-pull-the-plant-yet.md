You walked past the squash on Sunday and it looked like a squash plant. You
walked past it Wednesday and half of it looks like somebody came through with a
flour sifter. Grey-white patches on the upper side of the older leaves, a few
spots on the stems, and it is spreading toward the middle of the plant while you
stand there looking at it.

That is powdery mildew, it is early August, and it is doing the same thing in
every garden on your street this week. The instinct is to pull the plant and be
done with it. Hold off. The coated leaves are finished and nothing brings them
back, but a squash plant carrying powdery mildew will often keep setting fruit
for another three or four weeks. That is most of what is left of the season
anyway.

## This one ignores everything you know about watering

Most of the fungal trouble in a vegetable garden needs free water sitting on a
leaf before the spores can germinate. That is where all the standard advice
comes from: water at the base, water in the morning, keep the foliage dry.

Powdery mildew skips that step. Humid air is enough for it, which is why it
turns up in the middle of a dry stretch when nothing else is going wrong, and
why the neighbor who waters with a sprinkler at dusk somehow has less of it than
you do. Water sitting on the leaf surface works against these spores rather than
for them.

The air circulation part still holds, though. A squash plant in August is a
dense pile of overlapping leaves with dead air underneath, and that bottom layer
is always where it starts.

## Cut the worst leaves, then stop cutting

Go in with clean snips and take the leaves that are more than about half
covered. Cut the petiole down at the base rather than leaving a stub. Those old
lower leaves are producing spores by the million and contributing almost nothing
to the plant at this point, so pulling them out buys you airflow and lowers the
pressure on everything above.

Two things will tempt you into going further, and both are mistakes.

Do not take more than roughly a third of the canopy in one session. A plant that
just lost most of its leaves in August has to rebuild before it can ripen
anything, and you will lose more fruit to that than to the mildew.

And leave cover over the fruit. Squash sunscald is real and it happens fast on a
90 degree afternoon, especially on the shoulders of a winter squash that spent
its whole life shaded. A pale, papery, slightly sunken patch on the top of a
fruit is a leaf you should not have cut.

Carry what you cut out of the garden. A compost pile that genuinely heats will
handle it. Mine does not, so mine goes in the yard waste bin.

## What to spray, and what it can honestly do

Everything you can spray at this stage is a protectant. None of it cleans a
coated leaf. What it does is keep the new growth in the middle of the plant from
going the same way, which is the whole game from here.

Potassium bicarbonate is what I reach for first. It kills spores on contact,
tolerates heat better than most options, and does not smell like anything.
Horticultural oil and neem work by coating the surface, but neither should go on
above about 90 degrees or onto a plant that is already wilting in the heat, or
you will burn the leaves worse than the fungus would have. Sulfur is effective
and cheap with two hard rules attached: not above about 90 degrees, and never
within two weeks of an oil spray, because that combination scorches foliage.

I have tried the milk spray several times over the years with results I would
call inconsistent at best. Some seasons it seemed to slow things down. Other
seasons it did nothing I could see. I would not build a plan around it.

Whatever you use, get it onto the new leaves and the undersides, not just the
tops, and repeat it about weekly. Once a leaf is white it is a lost cause and
not worth the spray.

## The move most people miss in the first week of August

Summer squash runs somewhere around 45 to 55 days from seed to first picking.
Sown right now, a fresh zucchini or yellow squash can be producing by late
September across a good part of the country, on clean young leaves, in cooler
weather that mildew likes less. A sowing this late usually comes up after the
squash vine borer's main flight has passed, which is a second reason it tends to
do well.

Whether it works where you are is a first frost question and nothing else. Count
backward from your average first frost, add a week of margin, and see whether
the number still covers the days to maturity on the packet. Garden Pro Planner
keeps a planting calendar tuned to your local frost dates, which is the fastest
way to settle it.

Do not try this with winter squash. At 90 to 110 days it has no chance sown in
August anywhere with a real winter.

## When it is genuinely time to pull the plant

Check the base of the stem before you decide anything. If it is mushy, split, or
has what looks like wet orange sawdust packed around it, you are looking at
squash vine borer damage and the mildew was never the problem. That plant is
done, and it goes in the bin today rather than the compost.

Otherwise let it run until the fruit stops sizing up or the first frost takes
it. And while you are still annoyed enough to remember, write down which variety
this was. Plenty of modern squash and cucumber varieties carry real powdery
mildew resistance, and the difference between a resistant plant and a
susceptible one in a bad August is not subtle. In February, staring at a seed
catalog, you will have completely forgotten which one let you down.
