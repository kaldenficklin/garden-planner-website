Somewhere around now, with the garden asleep and the seed catalogues on their
way, a first-time gardener sits down with a pad of paper and draws three raised
beds. Sometimes four. A tomato bed, a salad bed, one for squash and beans, and
maybe a herb bed by the back door. It looks modest on paper, and it's exactly
the plan that most often ends with a weedy mess and a quiet decision not to
bother next year.

Build one bed. Plant it properly, keep it going from spring until frost, and
build the second one when the first has shown you what you're doing. I'd say
this to anyone starting out, and I'd have liked someone to say it to me.

## The garden you can look after is the garden you have

A vegetable bed in July wants water, and in a hot dry spell a raised bed can
want it every day or two. It wants the weeds pulled while they're small. It
wants someone to notice the first aphids on the underside of a leaf, the squash
that has stopped setting fruit, the lettuce that's about to bolt. None of that
is hard. All of it takes time, and the time scales with the ground.

The first spring, the time feels endless. The evenings are light, the beds are
new, and you're out there every night looking at seedlings. By the middle of
summer the novelty has worn off, there's a holiday, it's ninety degrees, and
each bed is another ten minutes with a hose. That's where a three-bed garden
starts to slip. Usually one bed gets the attention and the others get what's
left, and what's left isn't enough.

One bed fits into a life. You can water a single 4x8 bed in the time it takes
the kettle to boil, and you walk past every plant in it while you do. That
daily walk past is most of what good gardening is.

## The first year's real crop is information

You don't know your garden yet. Nobody does in their first season. You don't
know where the sun really falls in June compared with what you guessed in
November, or how fast your soil dries out, or which pest turns up first, or
whether the neighbour's cat considers your bed a toilet. You find out by
growing something there.

One bed is enough to learn all of that, and it's much easier to read. When the
tomatoes in your only bed split, you've got one set of conditions to think
about. When they split in one of three beds that were filled from different
places and watered on different days, you've got a puzzle.

And a mistake made in one bed is one bed's worth of mistake. I filled my first
bed with soil that turned out to be mostly fine sand, and the whole thing dried
out by lunchtime every day that summer. If I'd built three, I'd have made the
same mistake three times over.

## It costs less in the places that hurt

People price a raised bed by the timber, and the timber is the cheap part. A
4x8 bed a foot deep holds 32 cubic feet of soil, a little over a cubic yard,
and filling it with decent soil and compost usually costs more than the boards.
Three beds triple that bill before you've bought a seed.

There's a quieter cost too. A half-tended second and third bed grows weeds, and
weeds that go to seed in your first summer are weeds you'll be pulling for
years. One bed kept clean leaves you with a clean bed. Three beds kept
half-clean leave you a seed bank.

## A full bed will feed you more than you expect

The fear behind the three-bed plan is that one won't produce enough to be worth
the effort. It's a reasonable fear, and it mostly comes from picturing the bed
the way a first-timer plants it: one tomato in the middle, a row of lettuce, a
zucchini sprawled over everything else.

Planted closely and kept going, one bed does a lot. Lettuce and radishes in
spring, then something warm in the same squares once they're out. Bush beans
where a single zucchini would have smothered half the bed. A tomato or two
staked up the back so they take height instead of floor space. A bed planned
square by square, with the next crop ready to go in the day the last one comes
out, is busy from spring until frost. That comes from the planning, and a bigger
bed won't do it for you.

That's where a grid helps. Laying the bed out a square foot at a time, the way
the bed planner in
[Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) does, shows you
how much will fit, and its companion scoring flags the neighbours that
won't get along before you've put either of them in the ground. If you want a
sense of the numbers first, [this guide to what fits in a 4x8
bed](/blog/how-many-plants-actually-fit-in-a-4x8-bed-almost-everyone-guesses-high/)
is a good place to start.

## How you'll know it's time for the second bed

You'll know because you'll be annoyed. Somewhere in August you'll find
yourself wishing you'd had room for more of one particular thing: more beans
because you ate every one, space for winter squash you didn't have, a proper
row of garlic for next year. That wish is the best plan for a second bed there
is, because it comes from your kitchen rather than a catalogue.

The other sign is quieter. If the first bed made it through July and August
watered, weeded and still producing, without you dreading it, you have room in
your week for another. If it didn't, a second bed won't fix that. It'll double
it.

Build the second one next autumn, when the first year's garden has shown you
where the sun is and what you want more of. Fill it then, let it settle over
winter, and you'll go into your second spring with two beds you understand
instead of three you're hoping about.

For now, while it's cold and the catalogues are coming, draw one bed. Then
draw it again, smaller squares, more in it. That's the drawing that gets
planted.
