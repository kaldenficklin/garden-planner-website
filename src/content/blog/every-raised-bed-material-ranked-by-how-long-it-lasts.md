If you're building raised beds this winter, the material decides one thing
above everything else: how soon you'll be doing it again. Below is every common
option, longest-lasting first. The years are rough. A board's life depends
on how thick it is, how wet your climate is, and whether termites live near
you, so treat the numbers as the shape of the ranking rather than a promise.

| Rank | Material | Roughly how long it lasts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Stone, brick or concrete block | Decades; effectively permanent |
| 2 | Galvanised steel | Well over a decade |
| 3 | Composite or recycled plastic | Many years; doesn't rot |
| 4 | Modern pressure-treated lumber | Often ten years or more |
| 5 | Cedar, larch or other rot-resistant wood | Around a decade, sometimes much longer |
| 6 | Untreated pine or other softwood | A few years |
| 7 | Fabric beds | A few seasons |

## 1. Stone, brick or concrete block

Masonry doesn't rot or rust, and a well-built stone or brick bed will
outlast you. The trade is everything else. It's the slowest to build, the
hardest to change your mind about, and usually the most expensive unless you
already have a pile of reclaimed brick behind the shed.

Dry-stacked concrete blocks are the cheap way in. They go up in an afternoon,
and the hollow cores will hold a bit of soil for herbs or marigolds. Frost can
shift a dry-stacked wall over a few winters, so expect to nudge the odd block
back in line.

## 2. Galvanised steel

Corrugated steel beds, sold as kits or made from roofing panel and a timber
frame, last a long time. The zinc coating is what keeps the steel from
rusting, and once it wears through, usually at cut edges and scratches, rust
starts there. Kits with a coated or capped top edge tend to hold up better and
are kinder to your forearms.

Metal does get hot in full sun. In a deep bed full of damp soil I've never
noticed it matter much, but in a shallow one against a south-facing wall I'd
keep an eye on how fast the edges dry out.

## 3. Composite or recycled plastic

Boards made from recycled plastic, or plastic mixed with wood fibre, don't
rot and insects ignore them. They cost more than timber up front, and I don't
have a confident number for how long they last, since most of them haven't
been in gardens long enough to wear out. The weaknesses I've seen are sagging
and bowing. A long board with soil pushing on it from inside can belly
outward over a hot summer, so these beds want corner posts and a brace in the
middle of any long side.

## 4. Modern pressure-treated lumber

This is the one people ask about, so plainly: modern treated lumber is
generally considered fine for growing vegetables. The old arsenic-based
treatment (CCA) stopped being sold for residential use in the US at the end of
2003, and current treatments are copper-based. In the UK the equivalent is
usually sold as tanalised timber. Old railway sleepers and reclaimed timber of
unknown history are a different matter. Keep those out of food beds.

Some people still prefer to line the inside walls with heavy plastic sheeting
so soil never touches the wood. That's a reasonable choice if it gives you
peace of mind. Line the sides only and leave the bottom open, or you've built
a large pot with no drainage.

Buy boards rated for ground contact, not the cheaper above-ground grade. The
ground-contact rating is what gets you the longer life.

## 5. Cedar, larch and other rot-resistant wood

Western red cedar in the US, larch in the UK and plenty of places in between,
and redwood or oak where you can get them. The heartwood of these trees
resists rot without any treatment, which is why they're the default for
people who want wood and don't want treated lumber.

How long they last depends a great deal on the boards. Thick boards last much
longer than thin ones, and heartwood lasts much longer than the paler
sapwood near the bark. A bed of thin, sappy cedar fence pickets can be soft at
the corners in a handful of years. I built a bed like that once to save money
and learned that lesson exactly.

## 6. Untreated pine or other softwood

The cheapest timber and the shortest-lived. In a wet climate, or wherever
termites are busy, the boards can start to go within a few years, usually at
the corners and along the bottom edge where they sit on wet soil. In a dry
climate they can last a good deal longer.

For a first bed, a cheap pine box isn't a bad idea. You find out whether you
like the spot, the width and the height before spending on something
permanent. Just go in knowing you're renting it.

## 7. Fabric beds

Fabric raised beds and big grow bags are light, cheap and fold away. Sun
breaks the fabric down, so they're a few-seasons product, and the sides are
soft, so they dry out faster than any rigid bed. They're good for a balcony,
a rented garden or a trial year. They're a poor choice for anything you'd
like to still be there in five years.

## What shortens any bed's life

Contact with wet soil is what kills wood, so the bottom board goes first.
Setting a timber bed on a course of bricks or gravel, so it isn't sitting in
mud, buys it years. Thicker boards last longer than thin ones in every
material. Corners are where every kind of bed fails, because that's where the
soil pushes hardest and where water collects, so solid corner posts are worth
more than fancy boards.

If you're the sort who works out cost per year, the cheap options look less
cheap. A pine bed you rebuild twice in the life of one cedar bed is three
builds, three lots of screws, and three weekends of shovelling soil out and
back in. That's the part that makes me pay more for the long-lasting ones now.
