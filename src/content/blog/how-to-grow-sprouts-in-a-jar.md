Winter is a thin time for anything fresh and green from the garden, and a jar on the kitchen counter fills the gap faster than anything else. Mung beans, lentils, alfalfa and broccoli seed go from dry to edible in a few days, with no soil and very little light.

Before you start, though, there's food safety to be straight about.

## The risk, plainly

Sprouts are grown warm and wet, which is exactly how bacteria like to grow too. Raw and lightly cooked sprouts have been behind outbreaks of salmonella and E. coli, and the bacteria can be on or inside the seed itself, so growing them at home and rinsing them well doesn't take the risk away.

For most healthy adults it's a small risk. But the FDA advises children, older adults, pregnant women and anyone with a weakened immune system not to eat raw sprouts of any kind. Cooked sprouts are a different matter. Stir-fried until they're steaming hot all the way through, they're fine for everyone.

None of that means you shouldn't grow them. It means buy the right seed, keep everything clean, and throw out any batch the moment it smells wrong.

## Seed sold for sprouting

Buy seed sold for sprouting or for eating. Garden seed may be treated with fungicide (that's labelled on the packet, and treated seed is often dyed a bright colour) and isn't produced to food standards.

Stick to the usual ones: mung beans, green or brown lentils, alfalfa, broccoli, radish. Don't sprout red kidney beans to eat raw. They contain a toxin that only proper boiling destroys.

## Soak, rinse, drain

A tablespoon or two of small seed like alfalfa or broccoli is plenty for a quart jar, because they bulk up enormously. With mung beans and lentils use a bit more, around a quarter of a cup.

Soak the seed in cool water: a few hours for tiny seeds, overnight for beans and lentils. Cover the mouth of the jar with a sprouting lid or a square of muslin held on with a rubber band, pour off the soaking water, rinse with fresh water and drain again.

After that the routine is rinse and drain, morning and evening, every day. Leave the jar tipped mouth down at an angle in a bowl, so the last of the water runs out and air gets in. Water pooled at the bottom of the jar is what turns a batch slimy. Keep it at room temperature and out of direct sun, which heats the glass and cooks the sprouts.

## When they're ready

Lentils and mung beans are often ready in two to four days, when the tails are about as long as the bean. Alfalfa and broccoli take a little longer, four or five days, and a day in indirect light at the end greens up the tiny leaves. Taste one. Sweet and crunchy means done.

Give them a last rinse, drain them really well, let them dry on a clean towel, and keep them covered in the fridge. Eat them within a few days. Anything slimy, brown or musty goes on the compost heap.

Wash the jar and lid in hot soapy water between batches and start each one clean. I keep two jars going a couple of days apart, which is about right for one household's lunches through the winter.

**Pro tip.** Sprouts grow warm and wet, and so do bacteria. Raw sprouts have caused salmonella and E. coli outbreaks. Children, older people, pregnant women and anyone with weak immunity should eat them cooked.
