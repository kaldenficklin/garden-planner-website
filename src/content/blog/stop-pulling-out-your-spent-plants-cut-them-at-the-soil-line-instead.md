It's a late-October weekend and the beds look like a party nobody stayed
to clean up after. Tomato vines gone black from the first frost, bean plants
reduced to brown string, a row of peppers that quit the night the temperature
dropped. The reflex, and I had it for years, is to grab each plant at the base
and pull. The root ball comes up with a satisfying rip and a fistful of soil,
you knock it against your boot, and it goes in the barrow.

Don't pull them. Take a pair of pruners, cut each stem off where it meets the
soil, carry the top away, and leave the roots in the bed until spring.

## What actually comes up in your hand

The thing you pull out of the ground in October is the thick middle of a root
system. The fine roots that spread down and sideways through the bed all
summer snap off and stay where they are. So pulling doesn't even clear the
bed. It tears the centre out of each plant's roots, leaves the rest behind, and
churns up a circle of soil around every plant while it's at it.

That churning is what you want to avoid. A bed that has grown something all
summer is full of structure you can't see: channels left by roots and worms,
crumbs of soil held together by fungal threads and by the sticky stuff roots
and microbes give off. Yank a plant and you break a patch of that apart, and
you bring a layer of buried weed seed up into the light at the same time. Do
it to thirty plants and you've more or less dug the bed over.

Cut the plants instead and the roots rot where they grew. Worms and fungi work
through them all winter. By spring, the fine roots of a bean or a lettuce are
mostly gone, and what they leave is a channel running down into the bed, lined
with a bit of organic matter, more or less the path a new root would choose
anyway. Water follows those channels down rather than sitting on the surface.

There's a smaller reason too, and it matters most in a raised bed. Every root
ball you bang against the barrow sends a little soil off to the compost heap
with it. One plant's worth is nothing. It isn't the main reason beds sink a
couple of inches every year (that's organic matter breaking down), but it is
soil you bought by the bag, walking out of the bed one tomato at a time.

## About the beans

You'll hear that bean and pea roots should stay in the ground because the
small round nodules on them are full of nitrogen. A little, yes. But by the
time a bean plant has finished cropping, most of the nitrogen it fixed has
gone up into the pods, and you ate those in August. I leave bean roots in for
all the reasons above and count any nitrogen as a small bonus. I used to tell
people it was a free feed for next year's brassicas. It isn't much of one.

## How I clear a bed now

Bypass pruners do almost everything. Loppers handle the thick stuff: corn
stalks, sunflowers, the woody stumps of kale and Brussels sprouts. Cut flush
with the soil. For anything that might sprout again from what's left, like
chard or a kale stump with buds still on it, cut a little below the surface so
there's nothing left to leaf out.

Before the bed goes bare, it's worth writing down what grew where, because by
March the stubs will have softened into the soil and you'll be guessing. I
draw it on the square-foot bed grid in Garden Planner Pro, so next spring the
tomatoes go into squares that didn't have tomato roots rotting in them all
winter.

The tops go on the compost heap if they were healthy, chopped into short
lengths so they break down before next summer. Then the bed gets a blanket of
shredded leaves or compost over the top, stubs and all. Nobody sees the stubs
under that, and on mild days the worms come up to the mulch and pull it down
into the root channels you left them.

In spring, plant between the old stems. Most annual roots will have gone soft
and crumbly, and you can push a trowel straight through them. The woody ones,
corn and brassica stumps especially, can still be firm in April. Plant beside
them, or give them a twist; after a winter of rotting they usually come out
easily and bring very little soil with them.

## The plants that come out, roots and all

This is the part people skip, and it's the part that undoes the rest if you
get it wrong. A few things go out of the bed whole and into the bin, not the
compost heap.

Clubroot is the clearest case. If your cabbages, kale or Brussels sprouts
struggled and wilted on warm days, pull one up and look. Swollen, knotted,
distorted roots mean clubroot, and the spores in those roots stay alive in the
soil for many years. Leaving them to rot in the bed is putting the disease
back in the ground. Pull every brassica in that bed, bag the roots and bin
them.

Root-knot nematodes leave the same sort of evidence: lumpy, beaded swellings
along tomato, carrot or bean roots that don't rub off. Those roots come out
too.

Tomatoes that had early blight or septoria, the brown spots that started on
the bottom leaves and climbed, also come out whole. To be honest, the roots
aren't really the problem with those diseases; the stems and the fallen leaves
are, because that debris is where next year's spots start. I pull those plants
anyway, because it's the cleanest way to get every scrap of stem off the soil,
and I rake up the leaves and bag the lot. There's more on which garden waste
is worth worrying about in [the tomato cleanup post](/blog/the-tomato-plants-you-pull-this-week-decide-how-next-july-goes/).

Plants that died of fusarium or verticillium wilt can come out too, but don't
expect it to fix anything. Those fungi live in the soil on their own for
years, roots or no roots.

And perennial weeds. Bindweed, dock, couch grass, anything that came back from
a root. Cutting those at the soil line is how you get more of them, so dig
them out properly while the soil is soft.

Everything else, which in most beds is nearly everything, gets cut. It'll look
a bit unfinished for a week, a bed full of little brown stumps. Then the leaves
go on and it looks like any other bed in November, except the soil underneath
is still the soil you spent all summer building.
