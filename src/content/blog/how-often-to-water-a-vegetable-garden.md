More vegetable plants are killed by watering mistakes than by pests or disease. The tricky part is that both overwatering and underwatering show up as the same symptom — wilting, yellow leaves — so it's easy to "fix" the wrong problem. Here's how to actually get watering right this summer.

## The general rule: 1 inch per week

Most vegetable gardens need about **1 inch of water per week**, from rain or irrigation combined. That works out to roughly 0.6 gallons per square foot.

But that number is a starting point, not a schedule to follow blindly. How often you should actually water depends on:

- **Soil type** — sandy soil drains fast and may need water every 2–3 days; clay soil holds moisture and can go longer between waterings.
- **Weather** — hot, windy, or dry stretches pull moisture out of soil much faster than mild days.
- **Plant size and stage** — seedlings have shallow roots and dry out quickly; established plants with deep roots can go longer.
- **Container vs. in-ground** — pots and raised beds dry out faster than garden soil and often need daily water in summer heat.

## A better method: check the soil, not the calendar

Rather than watering on a fixed schedule, stick a finger 2 inches into the soil near your plants. If it's dry at that depth, water. If it's still moist, wait a day and check again.

This one habit prevents the two most common mistakes:

| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overwatering | Yellowing leaves, soft/mushy stems, fungus gnats, roots that look brown and mushy | Let soil dry out between waterings; improve drainage |
| Underwatering | Wilting (especially in the afternoon heat), crispy leaf edges, stunted growth | Water deeply and more often; add mulch to retain moisture |

## How to water for deep roots

**Water deeply and less often, rather than a light sprinkle every day.** A quick daily splash only wets the top inch of soil, which trains roots to stay shallow and makes plants more vulnerable to heat and drought stress.

Instead:

1. Water slowly, at the base of the plant, until the soil is moist several inches down.
2. Let the top inch or two of soil dry out before watering again.
3. Water in the morning when possible — it gives plants moisture to face the heat of the day and lets foliage dry before evening, which helps prevent fungal disease.

## Crops that are especially thirsty

Some vegetables are far more sensitive to inconsistent watering than others:

- **Tomatoes** — irregular watering causes blossom end rot and cracked fruit. Keep moisture level and consistent, especially once fruit sets.
- **Cucumbers and squash** — large leaves lose a lot of water; they wilt fast in heat and need reliable moisture to keep producing.
- **Lettuce and leafy greens** — shallow roots dry out quickly; inconsistent water makes them bitter and prone to bolting.
- **Peppers** — more drought-tolerant than tomatoes once established, but still need even moisture while fruiting.

## Cut your watering workload

A few habits make a real difference in how often you need to water at all:

- **Mulch.** A 2–3 inch layer of straw, shredded leaves, or bark around plants can cut soil moisture loss significantly and keeps roots cooler.
- **Water at the base, not overhead.** Soaker hoses or drip irrigation deliver water straight to the roots, waste less to evaporation, and keep foliage dry.
- **Group plants by water needs.** Thirsty crops like cucumbers next to drought-tolerant herbs means one or the other is always getting the wrong amount.

## The bottom line

Skip the rigid schedule — check the soil a couple inches down, water deeply when it's dry, and mulch to stretch the time between waterings. Consistency matters more than any exact number, especially for fruiting crops like tomatoes and cucumbers.
