Onion seed goes in while the garden is still asleep, which is half the appeal. Sometime in January or February, depending on where you are, a tray on the windowsill fills with green threads that look like very little for weeks.

It's slower than buying sets, and a bit fussier. But seed gives you a much wider choice of varieties, and seed-raised onions are less prone to bolting than sets, especially the big cheap sets. That's the reason I keep doing it, even in the years I swear I won't.

## Get the day length right before you buy

Onions start forming a bulb when the days reach a certain length, and varieties are bred for different lengths. This matters more than anything else on the packet.

Long-day onions need the long summer days of northern gardens: roughly the northern half of the US, Canada and the UK. Short-day onions bulb on shorter days and belong in the South. Plant a short-day onion up north and it bulbs too early and stays small. Plant a long-day onion in the South and the days never get long enough, so you get leaves and not much else. Intermediate, or day-neutral, varieties sit between the two and suit the middle band of the country.

One more thing for southern gardeners: short-day onions are often sown in autumn there, so the winter-indoors timing in this guide is mostly for everyone further north.

## Sowing, and the first few weeks

Count back 10 to 12 weeks from your last frost date. Garden Planner Pro's frost-date calendar will do the counting if you'd rather not.

Fill a tray or module cells with seed compost and sow thickly. Onions don't mind crowding at this stage, so a pinch of seeds per cell or a dense row in a tray is fine. Cover them thinly, about a quarter of an inch, and keep the tray somewhere warm until they come up, usually within a couple of weeks.

The seedlings come up bent double, like a green hairpin, and straighten over a few days. The moment you see them, they need light: the brightest window you have or, better, a grow light hung close above. Once they're up they're happy in a cooler room. Expect them to look thin and grassy for a long time. That's what onion seedlings look like.

## The haircut

When the tops get long and start flopping over, take a pair of scissors and cut them straight across to about 4 inches. Do it again every time they get long and floppy.

It feels wrong the first time. But trimmed seedlings stand upright, don't tangle into a mat, and put their effort into a thicker base. The trimmings smell wonderfully of onion and are perfectly good snipped over eggs, so nothing is wasted.

## Out into the cold

Onions are hardy, so they can go out a few weeks before your last frost once they've been hardened off. Give them a week or so outside by day in a sheltered spot, a bit longer each day, before they stay out for good.

Tease the clump apart gently, keeping as much root as you can, and set each seedling 4 to 6 inches apart, no deeper than it was growing in the tray. A bulb buried deep struggles to swell.

They'll lie flat and look half dead for the first few days after planting. Leave them alone and keep them watered. They stand back up on their own.

**Pro tip.** Buy onion seed fresh every year. It stays viable for less time than most vegetable seed, and an old packet is the usual reason a tray comes up thin and patchy.
