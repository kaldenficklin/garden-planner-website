The first time you prune a grapevine properly, you end up with a heap of cuttings on the ground and a few bare sticks on the wire, and it looks like you've killed it. You haven't. That's what a pruned grapevine looks like.

There are whole books on this, and commercial vineyards have systems with names. For one vine on a fence or a wire, you need much less than that.

## When to cut, and when not to

Prune when the vine is fully dormant: leaves down, wood bare, mid to late winter. In cold areas, later in the winter is better, after the worst of the cold, so you can see and remove anything the frost killed.

Don't leave it until spring. Once the sap starts rising, fresh cuts drip sap, sometimes for days. It's called bleeding, and while it doesn't usually kill a vine, it's a mess you can avoid by finishing before the buds swell. A reminder in Garden Planner Pro for late winter is an easy way not to miss the window.

## Which wood makes grapes

This is the one idea everything else hangs on. Grapes form on the green shoots that grow this spring, and those shoots come from buds on last year's canes.

So the wood you need is one year old. You can tell it apart easily. Last year's canes are smooth, brown and roughly pencil thick, with a bud at each joint. Older wood has rough, shaggy, peeling bark. The trunk and any permanent arms are old wood and stay, but they don't fruit directly.

Everything else about pruning is deciding how much one-year wood to keep and where.

## Two ways to do it

Spur pruning is the simplest to keep up. The vine has a permanent arm, or two, running along a wire. Each winter, every one-year cane growing from that arm gets cut back to a short spur with two buds. Those two buds make this year's fruiting shoots. Space the spurs out along the arm and take off any extras.

Cane pruning keeps longer pieces of one-year wood instead. Choose one or two healthy canes growing near the top of the trunk, shorten them, and tie them along the wire. Cut one more cane back to a two-bud spur near the trunk to grow next year's replacements, and remove the rest.

Most varieties are happy either way. Some fruit poorly from the buds close to the base of a cane and do better cane pruned, so check what the nursery says about yours.

## The heap on the ground

With either method, you'll take off the large majority of last year's growth. That's normal. A vine produces far more wood than it can turn into good fruit, and leaving too many buds gives you a crowded vine with lots of small bunches that ripen slowly.

Finish by cutting out any dead wood and pulling last year's tendrils off the wires. They grip hard and get in the way of tying in.

If the vine is young, the first couple of winters are about building the trunk and arms, not grapes. Pick one strong cane to become the trunk and cut the rest away.

**Pro tip.** Worried you've cut too much? You probably haven't. A vine left unpruned turns into a tangle carrying lots of small bunches that struggle to ripen.
