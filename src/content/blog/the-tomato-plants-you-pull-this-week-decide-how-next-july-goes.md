My tomato row looks like something that lost an argument. The bottom three feet
of every plant is bare stem, what leaves are left have gone yellow and spotted,
and there is a scatter of split fruit on the ground that the wasps found before
I did. Every year around now I stand there with a pair of loppers wondering how
much of this I am allowed to put on the compost heap.

The advice you hear is that anything diseased goes in the bin, full stop. That
is a fine rule if you want one rule. It is also wrong often enough to cost you a
lot of good compost, because a fair amount of what comes out of the garden in
September is either harmless or carries a problem that cleanup was never going
to solve.

## What is genuinely worth getting out of the bed

The two tomato diseases most of us end up with are septoria leaf spot and
early blight, and both of them spend the winter on the plant material you leave
behind. Septoria in particular lives on old tomato debris and on weeds in the
same family, then splashes back up onto the lower leaves of next year's plants
with the first hard rain. That is why the spots always start at the bottom of
the plant and climb. You are watering last year's crop residue onto this year's
seedlings.

So the stripped, spotted foliage is worth carrying out. Not because it will
infect the compost heap in some abstract way, but because leaving it lying in
the bed puts the spores three inches from where you will plant tomatoes again.
Get it out, and rake up the fallen leaves too. That is the part everyone skips.

Squash is the other one worth being fussy about. Squash bug adults overwinter
under whatever cover they can find, which means a pile of dead vines, a loose
sheet of mulch, or the board you left at the end of the bed in June. Vine borers
overwinter differently, as larvae or pupae in a cocoon an inch or two down in
the soil right at the base of the plant they killed. If a squash plant collapsed
on you this summer, the vine is worth removing and the soil around that crown is
worth disturbing.

## The one people have backwards

Late blight does not overwinter on your dead tomato vines. It cannot. The
organism needs living tissue to survive the winter, and a frosted, blackened
tomato stalk is not living tissue.

What it does survive in is potatoes. Cull piles, tubers you missed when you dug,
volunteers that come up in the same bed next May. That is the reservoir that
matters in a home garden, along with weather that carries spores up from
somewhere warmer. If you had late blight this year, the useful hour is not spent
bagging tomato vines. It is spent going back over the potato bed with a fork and
finding the ones you left behind.

Powdery mildew is the same kind of story in reverse. The white film on squash
leaves is fairly host-specific and, where winters actually freeze, it
does not persist on dead leaves in the garden. It blows in every summer whether
you cleaned up or not. Mildewed squash leaves can go on the heap.

And a lot of what looks like disease is not. Blossom end rot is a calcium
delivery problem inside the fruit. Sunscald is sunburn. Cracked and split
tomatoes are a watering story. Hornworm-chewed leaves carry nothing at all. All
of it composts fine.

## Wilt is a different problem and cleanup does not fix it

If a tomato plant yellowed from one side, wilted in the afternoon, and never
recovered, you may have had fusarium or verticillium wilt. Those live in the
soil for years on their own, with or without the plant. Pulling the plant is
sensible housekeeping, but do not tell yourself it fixed anything. What helps
there is rotation and buying varieties bred to resist it, which is a decision
you make in February with a seed catalog open.

Which is the real argument for naming what you had while the evidence is still
standing in front of you. Garden Pro Planner has pest and disease guides with
treatment notes for exactly this, and ten minutes with them now is worth more
than trying to remember in spring whether the spots had yellow halos.

## About your compost heap

The honest constraint is heat. A heap has to hold it, right through the
middle, for days, to reliably kill pathogens and weed seed. Turned piles that
you actively manage can get there. The slow heap in the corner that you add
kitchen scraps to does not, and even a hot pile has cool outer edges where
nothing gets cooked unless you turn the outside in.

So my working rule is that anything I would be upset to see again goes to
municipal green waste, where the piles are managed at a scale mine is not.
Everything else goes on the heap, and I have stopped feeling precious about it.

One last thing, and it is the mistake that undoes all of the above. Do not pull
everything out of the beds and stack it in a heap at the edge of the garden with
the intention of dealing with it later. That pile is warm, dry cover sitting ten
feet from next year's squash, and it is exactly what a squash bug is looking for
in October. Either it leaves the property this week or it goes on a heap you
will genuinely turn.
