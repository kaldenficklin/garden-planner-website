You go out to check the garden after three days of rain and half the ripe tomatoes look like they exploded from the inside. Deep cracks running down from the stem, sometimes rings circling the shoulder instead. Nothing ate them. Nothing attacked them. They just split.

A tomato's skin stops stretching once the fruit gets close to ripe, but the inside of the fruit doesn't stop taking up water. If the plant went dry for a stretch and then got hit with a heavy rain, or you finally remembered to water after forgetting for a week, the roots pull in water faster than the skin can expand to hold it. The inside swells, the skin can't keep up, and it gives. Picture an overfilled water balloon, just slower.

The dry-then-wet swing is the whole story. Consistent rain almost never does this. It's the whiplash, a week of cracked, dusty soil followed by two inches overnight, that splits fruit. I've had entire plants go through a soggy August without a single cracked tomato, and then split half the crop off one bad thunderstorm that arrived after ten dry days. The rain wasn't the problem. The gap before it was.

Some varieties are just more prone to it. Thin-skinned heirlooms and anything bred for size over durability, big beefsteaks especially, crack more than smaller, thicker-skinned varieties like most cherry and paste tomatoes. If you grow both side by side, watch how differently they handle the same storm. It won't be even.

## What helps

Splitting is a watering-consistency problem, not a rain problem, so the fix lives on the days it isn't raining. Deep, even watering a couple times a week beats a quick daily sprinkle that never reaches the roots and lets the soil dry out between waterings anyway. A few inches of mulch around the base does a lot of the work here too, since it slows how fast the soil dries out and evens out the swings you can't control from the sky.

If you know rain is coming and the soil under your plants has gone dry and cracked, a light watering ahead of the storm helps more than it hurts. It closes some of the gap between what the plant has been getting and what it's about to get dumped on it all at once, so the jump isn't as sharp.

Picking ripe fruit early, before a big storm, is the other real lever. A tomato that's already off the vine can't split from rain it never absorbs. If the forecast says heavy rain and you've got tomatoes at the pink or light-red stage, pull them. They'll finish ripening on the counter just fine, and you get to eat them instead of composting them.

Split tomatoes aren't a lost cause, by the way. If you catch them the same day, before anything gets into the crack, they're still fine to eat, just cut around the split and use them soon. It's the ones left out for a few days after splitting that turn into a problem, since the open crack is an easy way in for rot and fruit flies.

I check the forecast a lot more than I used to, mostly because of this. The Garden Pro Planner app's watering reminders catch the "it's been dry for a week and I forgot" half of the equation, which is the half I control. The rain, obviously, I don't get a vote on.
