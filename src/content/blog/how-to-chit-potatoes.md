Chitting is letting seed potatoes sprout before they go in the ground, standing them somewhere cool and light for about six weeks.

It is not compulsory, and a potato planted straight from the bag still grows. What it buys you is a couple of weeks of a head start, which on a first early is the difference between digging in June and digging in July.

## What it does and who it helps

A chitted potato goes into the ground already awake. Instead of spending its first fortnight underground deciding to sprout, it starts growing the week it is planted.

That matters most for first and second earlies, where the whole point is speed and where the crop is lifted before blight season gets going. Bringing harvest forward by two weeks is a real gain there.

It matters least for maincrop potatoes, which are in the ground for months anyway and will catch up on their own. Plenty of growers do not bother chitting maincrops at all, and that is a reasonable position rather than laziness.

It also does one useful thing regardless of type: it shows you which tubers are duds. A seed potato that sits for six weeks and produces nothing was never going to grow, and finding that out on a shelf is better than finding it out as a gap in the row.

## Buy the right potatoes

Use certified seed potatoes from a garden centre or supplier, not potatoes from the kitchen.

Certified stock is grown and inspected specifically to be free of the virus and blight diseases that build up in potatoes, and planting an infected tuber puts that into your soil for years. Supermarket potatoes are also often treated to suppress sprouting, so they may chit badly or not at all.

Pick tubers roughly the size of a hen's egg, firm, with no soft patches. Very large seed potatoes can be cut in half before planting, each half carrying at least two eyes, but let the cut surface dry for a day or two first and be aware that a cut tuber is more likely to rot in cold wet soil. Whole is simpler.

## Setting them up, and the mistake everyone makes

Look at a seed potato and you will find one end with a small scar where it broke off the parent plant, and the other end, blunter, with a cluster of eyes. That eye end is called the rose end and it goes upward.

Stand them in egg boxes, seed trays or a shallow cardboard box, one layer deep, not touching, rose end up. Then put them somewhere cool, frost free and well lit: an unheated porch, a spare room windowsill, a shed with a window.

The mistake is warmth and darkness. A cupboard, a garage with no window, an airing cupboard, all produce long, pale, brittle shoots that grew searching for light. Those snap off the instant you handle the tuber on planting day, and you have spent six weeks going backwards. What you want is the opposite: short, fat, dark green or purple shoots about an inch long, tough enough to survive being carried to the garden.

## How many shoots, and planting

By planting time each tuber may have half a dozen shoots or more. You can leave them all, or rub some off with a thumb.

Leaving everything gives you more stems, which gives you more potatoes, each of them smaller. Rubbing back to three or four strong shoots gives you fewer, larger potatoes. Neither is wrong; it depends whether you want salad potatoes or bakers.

When the time comes, handle them like eggs. Carry them in the tray rather than a bucket, set each one in the trench with the shoots pointing up, and draw soil over them gently. A shoot knocked off in the trench is not fatal, since the tuber usually produces another, but it has cost you the head start the whole exercise was for.

**Pro tip.** Cool and bright, not warm and dark. An airing cupboard gives you long white shoots that break the moment you handle them, which is worse than not chitting at all.
