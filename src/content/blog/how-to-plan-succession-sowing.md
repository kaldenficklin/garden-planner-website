Most vegetable gardens have a big burst in early summer and then patches of bare soil by August. Succession sowing is how you smooth that out, and winter is the time to plan it, while the beds are empty and you can think.

There are two halves to it. One is sowing the same quick crop little and often. The other is planning what goes into a bed once its first crop comes out.

## Little and often

Fast crops like lettuce, radishes and bush beans are the ones to stagger. Instead of one long row in spring, sow a short row, then another two or three weeks later, and keep going.

The short row is the hard part. It always feels like too little on the day. But a short row every couple of weeks means a few lettuces ready at a time for months, where one long row means twenty lettuces bolting in the same week. I've done that more times than I'd like.

Carrots, beets, spinach and cilantro all work this way too, as long as the weather suits them.

## What follows what

Plenty of crops finish by midsummer and leave a bed empty with months of growing time still left. Peas, garlic, early potatoes and overwintered onions are the usual ones.

Plan the follow-on crop now. Bush beans can go in after peas. A bed that held garlic can take autumn greens or a late sowing of carrots. Write both crops against the bed, with the rough date the first one comes out, so you're not standing in front of bare soil in July wondering what to do with it.

A rough sketch of each bed with a timeline down the side is enough. Mine is a pencil drawing that gets rubbed out a lot.

## Counting back from the first frost

Late sowings have a deadline: your first autumn frost. Take the days to maturity on the packet and count back from that date to find the last safe sowing.

Then add a couple of weeks. Days get shorter and cooler in autumn and everything grows slower than the packet assumes, which was written with summer in mind. Frost-tender crops like beans need to be finished before the frost. Hardy ones like kale and spinach can go later and stand a few cold nights.

## Summer heat and the seed order

Lettuce, spinach and cilantro bolt in hot weather. For midsummer sowings, choose varieties sold as slow to bolt, and sow where the bed gets some afternoon shade. A summer sowing in full sun on a hot week often comes up poorly or not at all.

Last, check your seed order against the plan. Six small sowings of lettuce use more seed than one, and running out of radish seed in June is an annoying way for the system to fall apart. Order the lot while you're planning, then put every sowing date on the calendar so the reminders arrive when you've long forgotten this winter's good intentions.

**Pro tip.** Sow less than feels sensible each time. A short row every couple of weeks gives a steady supply, and one long row gives you a glut and then nothing.
