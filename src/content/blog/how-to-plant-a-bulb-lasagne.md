If all you have is a balcony or a front step, you don't get a bed of tulips. You get a pot, maybe two. A bulb lasagne is how you make one pot do the work of a border.

The idea is simple. Bulbs that flower at different times go in at different depths, stacked in layers with compost between them, and they come up in turn from late winter into late spring.

## Which bulbs go where

The rule is that the last to flower goes deepest. Large late bulbs like tulips sit on the bottom layer. Daffodils, which flower earlier, go in the middle. Small early bulbs like crocus, grape hyacinths or dwarf iris go on top, close to the surface, and they're the first color you see.

You can swap things around. Hyacinths work in the middle layer, and some people do just two layers of tulips and crocus. What matters is that the big bulbs are deep and the small ones shallow, because a small bulb planted deep under a pile of compost may not make it up.

You'll worry that the shoots from the bottom will hit the bulbs above them. They don't get stuck. A shoot growing up from the bottom finds its way around anything in its path, the same way it gets past a stone in the ground.

## Building the pot

Use the deepest pot you can lift once it's full, and make sure it has drainage holes. Something around a foot deep or more gives room for three layers. Fill the bottom few inches with a gritty, free-draining potting mix.

Set the tulips on that, pointy end up, closer than you would in the ground. In a pot they can almost touch, and a crowded layer looks fuller in spring. Cover them with about two inches of compost. Lay the daffodils on that, nudging them into the gaps between the tulip tips below. Another layer of compost, then the small bulbs scattered over the top, then compost to an inch or so below the rim.

Water it once well so everything settles. Then leave it.

## Winter outside, but not frozen through

The pot needs to stay outdoors. Bulbs need a long cold spell to flower, and a warm room won't give them one. Keep the compost just damp through winter, and don't let the pot sit in a saucer of water.

The risk is a hard winter, when a pot freezes all the way through in a way the ground never does. Bulbs in the ground are insulated by everything around them; bulbs in a pot only have a thin wall. Where winters get properly cold, push the pot against a house wall, stand it on pot feet, and wrap it in burlap or bubble wrap during the worst spells. An unheated garage works too, if you bring it back out once the deep cold passes.

And then there's the waiting. Nothing happens for months. Then one morning in late winter there's a crocus in a pot you'd half forgotten, and the whole thing starts.

**Pro tip.** Squirrels dig pots too. Lay a piece of chicken wire on top of the last layer of compost and push the edges down inside the rim. The shoots grow straight through it.
