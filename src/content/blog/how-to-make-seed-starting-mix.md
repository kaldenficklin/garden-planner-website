Sometime in February you'll be standing in a garden centre looking at bags of seed compost, and the cheap ones will be full of lumps and the good ones will cost about what a tray of plug plants does. Mixing your own takes one dull November afternoon and a bin with a lid, and you know exactly what's in it.

It's less fussy than it sounds. A seed mix has a short job description: hold water, drain freely, stay soft enough for a root as thin as a hair, and not much else.

## Why garden soil fails in a tray

Put a trowel of good garden soil into a seed cell and water it a few times. It slumps, then sets into a dense plug that roots struggle through and water runs off. Out in the ground, soil has worms, frost and roots holding its structure open. In a little cell it loses all of that.

It also brings things in with it. Weed seeds come up alongside yours, and soil can carry the fungi behind damping off, where seedlings collapse at the base overnight. A warm, damp tray indoors gives those every advantage.

## A base, an opener, and not much food

The base holds water. Coir, which is coconut fibre, is the easy peat-free choice. It comes as compressed bricks that swell in a bucket of warm water to several times their size. Sieved garden compost works too, pushed through a fine riddle so nothing bigger than a lentil is left. Plenty of people use both.

The opener keeps air in the mix. Perlite is the white popcorn-looking stuff and mostly helps drainage. Vermiculite is the flaky golden one, and it holds water as well as letting air through. Either works, and a blend of the two is fine.

A common starting point is roughly equal parts coir, sieved compost and perlite or vermiculite, by volume. If your compost is weedy, leave it out and go with about two parts coir to one of vermiculite. None of these numbers is precise. You'll tweak it after a season of watching how your trays dry out.

## Why you don't want it rich

A seed carries its own food, enough to get it to its first true leaves. A mix loaded with fertiliser can scorch tender new roots and tends to give soft, leggy seedlings. So keep it lean: no added feed, no manure, no granular fertiliser.

Coir has very little in it, so once the first true leaves open, seedlings in a coir mix need potting on into richer compost or a weak liquid feed. Sieved garden compost carries a bit of food of its own, which is one reason it earns a place in the blend.

## Wet it before it goes in the cells

Dry mix is surprisingly hard to wet. Water poured on top runs down the sides of each cell and out the bottom, leaving a dry core you can't see. Tip the mix into a bucket, add warm water a little at a time, and work it through with a trowel until it's evenly damp, about like a sponge you've wrung out.

Then fill the trays loosely, tap them on the bench a couple of times to settle the mix, and scrape the top level just below the rim. Don't press it down hard. Seedlings want it springy.

Make up the bulk now and keep it dry in a bin or a sack with a lid, out of the rain, and wet only what you need on sowing day. Damp mix left sitting in a warm shed for weeks can grow mould and moss on top, and I'd rather not start a spring of seedlings on that.

**Pro tip.** Keep a small bag of the finest sieved mix, or plain vermiculite, for a thin covering over seeds after sowing. It stays loose so seedlings push through it easily.
