Pea shoots are about the sweetest thing you can grow on a windowsill in winter. They taste of fresh peas, crunchy stem and soft leaves, and a tray is usually ready to cut two to four weeks after sowing, depending on how warm the room is.

They also cost almost nothing. A bag of whole dried peas from the grocery store grows exactly the same shoots as seed sold in little packets for the purpose, as long as you buy the right kind.

## The right peas

Whole dried peas are what you want, the kind sold for soups, or dried marrowfat peas. Split peas won't grow, because splitting breaks the seed in half and damages the part that would have sprouted. If you'd rather use garden seed peas, check the packet says untreated, since some seed peas are coated in fungicide.

And make sure they're peas. Sweet peas, the flowering climbers, are a different plant, and their seeds and shoots are poisonous. Never grow sweet pea shoots to eat, and keep the two kinds of seed apart in the shed.

## Soaking and sowing

Put the peas in a bowl, cover them with plenty of cool water and leave them overnight. They swell as they drink, so give them more water than looks necessary. Drain them in the morning.

Fill a seed tray, or any shallow container with drainage holes, with a few centimetres of damp compost. Spread the soaked peas over the surface so they touch, in one layer with no gaps, and press them in. A thin sprinkle of compost on top is optional. They'll grow without it, but it stops them drying out in a warm room.

## On the windowsill

Put the tray on the brightest windowsill you have. Winter light is weak, and in a dim corner the shoots come up pale and floppy and fall over. Turn the tray every couple of days so they don't all lean toward the glass.

Keep the compost damp but never soggy. Watering from below, by standing the tray in a dish of water for a few minutes, keeps the leaves dry. Mould is the usual problem with any densely sown tray, and a bit of air moving around helps more than anything. That's easy to forget in a shut-up house in November, so open a window nearby on milder days.

## Cutting, and cutting again

Cut when the shoots are 8 to 10 cm tall, about three or four inches, with a few sets of leaves and perhaps the first curly tendril. Use scissors and cut just above the lowest leaf rather than down at the compost.

That leaf is what lets the plant regrow. A tray will often give you a second, smaller crop a couple of weeks later. The second cut tends to be thinner and a little tougher, and after that the peas have run out of steam, so tip the lot on the compost heap and start again.

Eat them raw in a salad, piled on soup, or tossed into a stir-fry for the last minute. If you'd like a steady supply, sow a new tray every week or ten days.

**Pro tip.** Edible peas only. Sweet peas, the flowering kind, are a different plant and their seeds and shoots are poisonous. Keep those seed packets well away from the kitchen.
