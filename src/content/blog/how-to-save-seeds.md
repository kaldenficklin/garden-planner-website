Saving your own seed is one of those things that sounds like a serious undertaking and is mostly not. For beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce it amounts to leaving something on the plant slightly too long and then drying it properly.

There is one decision that matters more than any technique, though, and it happens before you plant anything.

## Hybrid or open-pollinated decides the whole thing

Look at the seed packet. If it says F1, that variety is a hybrid, a deliberate cross between two parent lines. The plant you grow is uniform and often very good, but the seed it sets does not come true. Sow it and you get a scattered mix of the grandparents, some of it poor, none of it what you saved it for.

Open-pollinated varieties, which includes everything sold as heirloom, do come true. Save seed from a good open-pollinated tomato and you get that tomato again next year.

So if seed saving appeals to you, buy open-pollinated varieties in the first place. There is nothing wrong with growing F1 plants, they are just a dead end for this particular purpose.

Save from your best plants, not the leftovers. The strongest plant, the one that cropped earliest or resisted disease best, is the one whose seed you want.

## Some crops cross, and some do not

Beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce mostly pollinate themselves before the flower even opens. You can grow several varieties near each other and still save seed that comes true. That is why they are the ones to start with.

Squash, pumpkins, courgettes, brassicas, sweetcorn and most of the cucumber family are the opposite. They are pollinated by insects or wind, they cross with any related variety in the neighbourhood, and the seed you save can turn into something unrecognisable. This is where the stories of unexpectedly bitter squash come from.

To save from those you need real separation distance, or you have to bag individual flowers and hand-pollinate them yourself. Worth doing eventually, but not where anyone should begin.

## Let it go further than you would for eating

Seed is not finished when the vegetable is good to eat. A cucumber for seed stays on the vine until it is fat and yellow. A bean pod stays until it is brown, papery and rattles. Lettuce has to run up and flower and then form fluffy seed heads, which takes weeks and looks like a plant you should have pulled.

Set aside one or two plants specifically for this and accept you are not eating those. Trying to get both a crop and seed from the same plant is where people give up.

Tomatoes are the exception with an extra step. Squeeze the seeds and their jelly into a jar with a little water and leave it somewhere warm for three or four days until it smells sour and grows a film on top. That fermentation strips off the gel coating that stops the seed germinating. Then rinse in a sieve until clean, and the good seed sinks while the dead ones float.

## Drying and storage do most of the work

Seed that goes into storage even slightly damp will either mould or quietly lose its viability, and you find out in spring when nothing comes up.

Spread the seed in a single layer on a plate or a sheet of paper, somewhere airy and out of direct sun, and give it a week or two. A dry bean should snap rather than bend. A dry tomato seed should not stick to your finger.

Then into paper envelopes, into a sealed jar, and somewhere cool and dark and stable. A cupboard indoors is better than a shed, where the temperature and damp swing all year. Most vegetable seed stays good for three to five years like that, and beans and lettuce often longer.

Next spring, before you commit a whole row to it, put ten seeds on damp kitchen paper in a bag for a week and see how many sprout. That tells you what you have.

**Pro tip.** Write the variety and the year on the envelope before you fill it. Every gardener who has promised to label seeds later has ended up with a drawer of identical brown envelopes and no idea what is in any of them.
