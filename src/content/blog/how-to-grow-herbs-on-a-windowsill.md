A row of herbs on the kitchen windowsill is one of the nicer things about winter, when the garden has nothing to pick. It's also where a lot of herbs quietly die, and it's almost never the cold that does it. It's the light, or the watering.

The herbs that last are the ones chosen for a dim season and left a little dry.

## Light decides almost everything

Winter sun is low and short, and a window that blazes in July is gentle in December. Give the herbs the brightest window you have. In the Northern Hemisphere that's a south-facing one; east or west will do for the tougher herbs. A north window is too dark for most of them.

Chives, parsley, mint and thyme are the most forgiving. They'll grow slowly, but they'll keep giving you something to cut. Rosemary and oregano can manage in a very bright spot.

Basil is the hard one. It wants long, bright days and warmth, and it sulks on a cool sill in midwinter. If you love it enough, put it in the sunniest window you have or under a grow light, keep it away from cold glass and drafty doors, and expect it to be a little sparse anyway. I grow it every winter and I'm still never quite happy with it.

## Pots and water

Every pot needs a drainage hole. Decorative pots without one fill up at the bottom, the roots sit in water, and the herb rots from the base. If you want the pretty pot, keep the herb in a plain plastic pot with holes inside it, and tip out any water that collects.

Water less than you would outdoors in summer. Herbs indoors in winter grow slowly and use very little. Push a finger into the compost, and water when the top inch or so is dry. Mint and parsley like it a bit damper; thyme and rosemary would rather be on the dry side.

Turn the pots every few days. Herbs on a sill lean hard toward the glass, and a quarter turn keeps them upright and growing evenly.

## The trouble with supermarket herbs

Those pots of basil and parsley from the grocery store are sown thick, with dozens of seedlings crammed into one pot, so they look full on the shelf. On a windowsill they fight each other and collapse within a few weeks.

Tip one out and gently pull the root ball into three or four smaller clumps. Pot each one up in fresh compost, water it, and give it a week to settle. You get several plants that last instead of one that doesn't.

## Harvest a little at a time

Indoors in winter a herb grows back slowly, so take a few stems at a time and never more than about a third of the plant. Cut just above a pair of leaves and it will branch from there.

If a plant stops putting out new growth for weeks, it usually wants more light, not more water or feed. Move it to a brighter spot before you do anything else.

**Pro tip.** On frosty nights, pull the pots back from the glass or tuck them behind the curtain line. Leaves pressed against a cold window can be damaged even in a warm room.
