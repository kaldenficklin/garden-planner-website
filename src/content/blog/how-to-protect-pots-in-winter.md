It's a familiar spring disappointment. The lavender in the border came through the winter fine, and the identical one in a pot by the door is dead. Same plant, same garden, same cold.

The difference is the pot. A plant's roots are much less cold-hardy than its stems and leaves, because in the ground they never need to be. The soil around them stays warmer than the air. In a pot, the roots have a thin wall between them and the weather, and they can freeze solid on a night the border barely notices.

## Choose plants that can take it

If a pot is going to stay outside all year, the plant in it should be tougher than your climate strictly needs. A common rule of thumb is to pick plants rated at least a zone or two colder than yours. It's not exact, but it's a good way to stop losing the same shrub every February.

Bigger pots help too. More compost takes longer to freeze through, so a large container gives roots more of a buffer than a small one.

## Group, raise and wrap

Pots lined up singly along a path are about as exposed as they can be. Move them together against a house wall, ideally a side that gets some sun and is out of the prevailing wind. A house wall gives off a little warmth, and pots huddled together shelter each other.

Raise them on pot feet or a couple of bricks. Pots sitting flat on a patio drain badly, and in winter that means roots sitting in water that then freezes.

Then wrap the pots themselves in burlap, bubble wrap or old blankets, tied around the outside. The point is to insulate the root ball, so wrap the pot and leave the plant's top alone. Most hardy plants need light and air on their leaves even in winter.

## Terracotta and water

Unglazed terracotta soaks up water, and when that water freezes it can flake or crack the pot. Some terracotta is sold as frost-resistant and holds up well, but cheap pots often don't. I've lost a few nice ones I thought were tough. Empty terracotta goes into the shed for winter. Planted terracotta is worth moving into shelter during the coldest spells.

Watering sounds wrong in winter, but moist compost holds heat better than dry, so a pot watered the day before a hard freeze is better off than a dry one. The opposite problem is just as real: pots left sitting in full saucers. Empty every saucer before winter, or take them away entirely.

## When it gets really cold

For the handful of nights a winter where it drops well below normal, move the most precious pots into an unheated garage or shed. It doesn't need to be warm; it only needs to stop the pot freezing all the way through. A few days in the dark won't hurt a dormant plant.

Bring them back out as soon as the deep cold passes. A warm, dim garage for weeks on end is how you get a plant that wakes up too early and grows weak, pale shoots.

**Pro tip.** Evergreens in pots keep losing water through their leaves all winter, even when the compost is frozen. Water them on mild days when the pot has thawed.
