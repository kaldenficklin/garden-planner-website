A month ago you carried the geraniums in before the first frost, and they
looked great. Now the stems have stretched out toward the window, there's a
long bare gap between each leaf, the new leaves are small and a washed-out
yellow-green, and the old ones at the bottom keep turning yellow and dropping
on the sill.

Nothing is eating them and they're not dying. They're short of light, and
almost always they're also too warm, and the combination makes them grow
faster than the light can support. That's fixable, and the stretched growth
you've already got can be cut off in late winter, so the plant you put out
in spring needn't look anything like the one on your windowsill today.

These are the zonal geraniums most of us grow in pots and window boxes, the
ones with round, scalloped leaves (often with a darker band) and big
clusters of red, pink or white flowers. Strictly speaking they're
pelargoniums, but everyone calls them geraniums.

## Light is most of it

Indoor light in winter is far weaker than anything a geranium got outside
over the summer, even on a sunny windowsill. The days are short and the sun sits
low. Window glass takes a bit more. A geranium responds the way most plants
do: it puts its energy into growing longer stems to reach the light, and it
skimps on the leaves. The pale colour is the plant making new growth faster
than it can make the green to go with it.

Look at which way the stems lean. If they're all bent hard toward the glass,
light is your main problem.

## Warmth makes it worse

This is the half people miss. A warm room tells the plant to keep growing,
but the light isn't there to grow properly with, so you get more of that
thin, stretched growth. In a cool room the plant slows right down and mostly
waits for spring, which is what you want.

Geraniums overwinter best somewhere cool and bright, roughly in the 50s to
low 60s °F (about 10 to 16 °C). A living room near a radiator or a heating
vent is about the worst spot in the house for them, even though it feels like
the kindest one.

## Too much water and feed

Watering on the summer schedule is the other common cause. A slow-growing
geranium in a cool room uses very little water, and soil that stays damp all
the time leads to yellowing lower leaves and, at worst, a soft, black rot at
the base of the stem. If a stem is black and squishy where it meets the soil,
that one is gone. Cut it out, take a cutting from any healthy tip, and let the
rest of the pot dry out properly.

Feeding pushes growth the plant can't support in this light, so stop until
spring.

## What to do this week

Move the plants to your brightest window. In the Northern Hemisphere that's
usually one facing south. Get them right up close to the glass, since light
drops off quickly as you move back into the room, but keep the leaves from
touching a pane that gets icy.

Then find them a cooler spot if you can: an unheated spare room, a cool
porch that doesn't freeze, a bright window in a hallway. If the brightest
window is in the warmest room, that's a trade-off, and on balance I'd keep
the light.

Turn the pots a quarter-turn every week or so, so they don't all lean one
way.

Water less. Push a finger into the soil, and water only when it's dry an inch
or so down, then water through and let the pot drain. In a cool room that
might be every couple of weeks.

If you have a seedling light, a geranium under it for the winter will stay
far more compact. I don't bother for mine, because I'm cutting them back
anyway, but it's the one thing that would stop the stretching outright.

Pick off the yellow leaves as they go. A few lower leaves dropping over
winter is normal and not worth worrying about. Check the leaf undersides
while you're there, because whiteflies sometimes ride indoors on geraniums,
and a cloud of tiny white specks when you brush the plant means you've got
them.

## Pinch back in late winter

Don't try to cut the plant into shape now. In this light it will only grow
back stretched. Wait until late winter, around February or early March, when
the days are noticeably longer.

Then cut each leggy stem back hard, by about half or more, to just above a
leaf joint (the slightly swollen node where a leaf grows or grew). New shoots
come from those nodes, so a stem cut low gives you a bushy plant instead of a
tall one. It looks brutal. I hesitated over it for years, and every year the
plants I cut hardest were the best ones on the balcony in June.

Once you see new growth, you can start watering a bit more and give a dilute
feed every few weeks.

## Take cuttings from what you cut off

The tips you cut off are free plants. Take a piece three or four inches long
with a couple of leaves at the top, strip off the lower leaves, and leave it
on the counter for a few hours so the cut end can dry. Then push it into a
small pot of gritty, free-draining mix and keep it on a bright windowsill.

Don't cover geranium cuttings with a plastic bag the way you would for a lot
of plants. They rot easily in humid air, and they root fine without it. Water
lightly and wait. There's more detail on the method in the
[cuttings guide](/blog/how-to-take-plant-cuttings/).

A cutting rooted in March is often a better-looking plant by May than the
leggy old one it came from.

## Water less than feels right

If you change only one thing, water less.
Light is hard to fix in winter and warmth often is too, but water is entirely
up to you. The watering reminders in
[Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) are handy for
this: set one to remind you to check the pots, then only water the ones that
are dry.

And if the stems are already long and pale, leave them be. They're doing no
harm, and in February they're the cuttings.
