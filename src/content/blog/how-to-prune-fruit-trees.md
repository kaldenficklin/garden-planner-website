Pruning a fruit tree frightens people more than any other garden job, and the fear is mostly misplaced. An apple tree is very hard to kill with secateurs.

What you can do is get the season wrong on the wrong tree, which is a real mistake and the first thing worth sorting out.

## Which tree, which season

Apples and pears are pruned in winter, when the tree is fully dormant, the leaves are long gone and you can see the whole shape. Anywhere from after leaf fall to just before the buds break.

Plums, cherries, apricots, peaches and all the other stone fruit are the opposite, and this is the part that matters. They are pruned in summer, while they are growing strongly, because that is when a wound seals fastest. A winter cut on a plum sits open and wet for months, and it is the classic way that silver leaf and bacterial canker get into a tree. You can lose the whole tree to that, which is a different order of mistake from a badly shaped apple.

If you take one thing away: apples and pears now, stone fruit in July.

Newly planted trees are their own subject, shaped over the first three or four years to build a framework. What follows is maintenance pruning of an established tree.

## The order to work in

Stand back and look at the tree for a minute before cutting anything. It is genuinely the most useful part of the job and the part everyone skips.

Then work through it in order. First the three Ds: dead wood, damaged wood, diseased wood. That alone improves most neglected trees and there is no judgement involved, because none of it was doing anything.

Second, anything crossing or rubbing. Two branches grinding against each other in the wind wear a wound that never closes, so take out whichever is weaker or worse placed.

Third, the middle. A fruit tree wants light and air through the centre, roughly the shape of an open goblet or a wine glass, because fruit ripens where light reaches and disease settles where air does not move. Take out branches growing straight up through the middle and anything heading back into the centre.

Fourth, and only if there is more to do, shorten what is left.

## How to make the cut

Two kinds of cut, and both have a rule.

Shortening a shoot: cut just above a bud, about a quarter inch up, with the cut sloping away from the bud so water runs off it. Choose a bud pointing outward, away from the middle of the tree, because that is the direction the new shoot will grow.

Removing a whole branch: cut just outside the branch collar, the slightly swollen ring of bark where the branch meets the trunk. That collar is the tree's own sealing tissue. Cutting flush to the trunk removes it and leaves a wound that takes years to close, and leaving a long stub gives you a piece of dead wood that rots back toward the trunk.

Do not paint the cuts with anything. Wound sealants were standard advice for a long time and are now understood to trap moisture and slow the tree's own response. A clean cut left open is better.

Use sharp tools, and wipe the blades between trees if you are cutting anything diseased.

## How much, and what happens if you take too much

A quarter of the canopy in one winter is the usual limit, and less is safer.

Go beyond that and the tree responds to the loss by throwing up water shoots: vertical, whippy growth straight out of the main limbs, which bears no fruit and blocks all the light you just let in. Then you prune those out the following year, which provokes more. A badly overgrown tree is brought back over three or four winters, not one.

If you are unsure, do less. An under-pruned tree still gives you fruit. The nervous beginner's mistake is almost never that they cut too little.

**Pro tip.** Prune plums, cherries and apricots in summer, never in winter. Winter cuts on stone fruit are how silver leaf and bacterial canker get in, and that is a tree you lose rather than a crop.
