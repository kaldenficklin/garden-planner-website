An amaryllis is about the most dependable flower you can grow in the middle of winter. Pot one up in November and it will usually bloom somewhere between six and ten weeks later, a stalk as thick as your thumb carrying a handful of huge trumpets, on a windowsill, while everything outside is brown.

The bulbs are boxed up in every supermarket this time of year. Strictly they're Hippeastrum (the true Amaryllis is a different South African plant), but everyone calls them amaryllis and so will I. A few small things decide whether yours flowers well, and whether it flowers again next winter.

## Pick a fat, firm bulb

Bigger bulbs tend to send up more flower stalks. Give it a squeeze. It should feel firm and heavy, like a good onion, with no soft patches and no mould around the base. A green or red tip poking out of the neck is fine and means it's keen to get going.

The waxed bulbs sold as no-water decorations will flower on their own stored energy, but the wax seals them off from roots, and they rarely make it to a second year. Treat one as a long-lasting cut flower and you won't be disappointed.

## A pot only a little wider than the bulb

Amaryllis likes to be snug. Choose a pot that leaves about an inch, two or three centimetres, between the bulb and the rim all the way round. It needs a drainage hole, and something heavy like terracotta helps, because a tall flower stalk will tip a light plastic pot over.

Set the bulb so the top third, the shoulders and the neck, sits above the compost, and firm the compost in around it. Any decent multipurpose or potting compost is fine. Try not to bury the neck, which can rot if it sits wet.

## Barely water until it moves

This is the step most people get wrong. A bulb with no leaves can't use much water, and a pot kept wet before the roots get going is how bulbs rot. Water once after planting, let it drain, then leave it alone until you see a shoot or a flower bud. After that, water when the top of the compost is dry, and tip out the saucer so it never stands in water.

Warmth wakes it up, so a bright spot in a heated room is right. The flower stalk grows toward the light, and fast, so give the pot a quarter turn every few days to keep it straight. If it leans anyway, push a thin cane into the compost beside the bulb, not through it, and tie the stalk loosely.

Once the flowers open, moving the plant somewhere a little cooler and out of direct sun makes them last longer.

## Cats, dogs and amaryllis

Every part of an amaryllis is toxic to cats and dogs, and the bulb is the worst part. Chewing on it can cause vomiting, drooling and diarrhoea. If you have a pet that chews houseplants, put the pot somewhere it truly can't reach, and sweep up dropped petals and leaves. If you think your pet has eaten some, call your vet. It isn't something for small children to chew on either.

## Keeping it for next winter

When the last flower fades, cut the stalk off a couple of inches above the bulb and leave the leaves. They're what refill the bulb for next year. The long strap leaves that come after flowering look untidy on a windowsill, but they do all the work.

Keep it in your brightest window, water when the compost is dry, and feed every couple of weeks with a liquid houseplant or tomato feed. After your last frost it can go outside for the summer, still in its pot, somewhere sunny that isn't baking.

In late summer or early autumn, stop watering and let the leaves yellow. Cut them off and keep the bulb in its pot somewhere cool, dark and dry for about two months. Then bring it back into warmth and light, water once, and wait for the tip to move. I won't pretend every bulb reblooms. Some years mine sulk and put up only leaves. But a bulb that was fed well all summer usually does.

**Pro tip.** Amaryllis is toxic to cats and dogs, the bulb most of all. If you have a pet that chews plants, keep the pot out of reach and pick up fallen petals and leaves.
