The maples are going this week. If you have a tree or two, you are about to have
more organic matter lying on your lawn than you could buy in a season, and the
default thing to do with it is rake it into paper sacks and leave it at the
curb for someone to take away.

I did that for years. Then I priced a few bags of compost one spring, looked
at the pile of sacks on the curb next door, and felt a bit stupid. Leaves are
the cheapest soil improver most of us will ever be handed, and the season for
collecting them is about three weeks long. These are the eight things I do
with them now, roughly in the order the year asks for them.

## 1. Run the mower over them first

Nearly everything below works better with shredded leaves than whole ones.
Whole leaves, especially big flat ones like maple and sycamore, lie on top of
each other when they get wet and form a sheet that water runs off and air
can't get through. In spring you lift a mat like that and find cold, sour soil
underneath and a lot of slugs.

You don't need a shredder. Spread the leaves out on the lawn a few inches deep
and go over them with a mower that has a bag on it, once or twice. They come
out a quarter of the size, mixed with a bit of grass, and they break down far
faster. If a leaf blower in your shed has a vacuum setting, that shreds too.

## 2. Cover the beds you've just cleared

Any bed you've pulled the summer crops out of and not planted with garlic or
a cover crop is bare soil going into winter. Bare soil loses its structure in
heavy rain and grows a crop of weeds in March. A few inches of shredded leaves
stops both.

By spring the bottom layer will be half gone into the soil, and the worms do
most of that work for you. Rake back what's left when you want to sow, and it
goes back on as mulch once the seedlings are up. If you're anxious about
leaves blowing away, a light sprinkle of compost or grass clippings on top
weighs them down.

## 3. Start a leaf mould pile you'll forget about

Leaf mould is what you get when leaves are left to rot slowly and on their
own, without the greens a compost heap needs. It takes a year at the least and
often two, and what comes out is dark, crumbly, smells like a wood floor, and
holds water like a sponge. I use it in seed-sowing mixes and on top of the
beds where I grow carrots.

A ring of chicken wire or a few pallets in a corner is enough. Fill it with
leaves, wet them thoroughly if they went in dry, and walk away. Some people
stuff damp leaves into black bin bags, poke a few holes in them and stack them
behind the shed, which works too and takes up less room.

## 4. Keep a few bags dry for next summer

This is the one I wish someone had told me sooner. A compost heap needs browns
as well as greens, and in July you have nothing brown. You have grass
clippings, pulled weeds, bolted lettuce and the kitchen scraps, and the heap
goes slimy and starts to smell.

So fill four or five sacks with dry leaves now and keep them somewhere the
rain can't get in. In summer, a few handfuls go on with every bucket of greens.
It costs nothing and it's the difference between a heap that rots sweetly and
one the neighbours mention.

## 5. Tuck in the roots you're leaving in the ground

If you've got carrots, parsnips or leeks you plan to dig through the winter,
the problem is not the cold itself. It's the ground freezing solid so that you
can't get a fork in. A deep blanket of leaves over the row, a foot deep or more
and held down with something, keeps the soil underneath workable for a good
while longer.

Mark the row before you cover it. I once spent a January afternoon digging
three trial holes in the wrong place, in the dark, for one leek.

## 6. Mulch the garlic, but not today

Garlic that went in this month will want a mulch over the winter, mostly to
stop the freezing and thawing pushing the cloves up out of the soil. Shredded
leaves are good for it, a few inches deep.

The timing matters a bit. Wait until the soil has properly cooled and the
first hard frosts have come, so you're keeping the ground cold rather than
keeping it warm for voles. Until then, keep the bagged leaves standing by.

## 7. Build next year's bed on top of the lawn

If you want another bed next spring and don't fancy lifting turf, you can make
it now with leaves. Mow the grass short, lay cardboard over it with the edges
overlapping well, soak it, then pile shredded leaves on top, as deep as you've
got, with a few inches of compost or manure over the lot.

Over the winter the grass dies under the cardboard and the leaves start to
break down. By spring you can plant into the compost layer, though I'd put in
transplants and big seeds that first year rather than carrots, because the
layer underneath will still be lumpy. This is the method I'd pick if I were
starting a garden again on a lawn.

## 8. Pack them round the pots that stay outside

If you've got a hardy herb or a small fruit bush living in a container on a
patio or a balcony, the roots are much more exposed in a pot than they would
be in the ground. Push the pots together against a house wall and stuff bagged
leaves into the gaps and around the outside. It's a scruffy look for a few
months, and it can be the difference between a pot that comes back in April
and one that doesn't.

## The leaves I leave out

Most leaves are fine. The worry about oak leaves turning your soil acid is
overblown in my experience; by the time they've broken down there's not much
in it. The one tree I don't bring into the vegetable beds is
[black walnut](/blog/black-walnut-tree-toxic-to-vegetable-gardens/).
Its roots and hulls are the main worry, but the leaves carry some of the same
compound, and I'd rather they rotted down somewhere else first.

And whichever beds get leaves this autumn, write down which ones. Next spring
you'll be wondering why one bed drains better and grows better than the
others, and it helps to know. A line in the garden journal in
[Garden Planner Pro](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1539031278) is still there
next autumn, and the one after, which is longer than I can be trusted to
remember a pile of leaves.
